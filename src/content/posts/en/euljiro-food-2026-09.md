Euljiro's food grew up around the working hours of the printers and tool shops that filled the district. According to the Korea Tourism Organization (KTO), the nogari alley began in **November 1980 with the first nogari beer pub, Eulji OB Bear**, and grew as print workers coming off night shifts stopped for draught beer on the way home. Long before that, in 1946, Woo Lae Oak started serving Pyongyang-style cold noodles near the Euljiro 4-ga rotary, and by around 1953 a Hamheung-style noodle house had opened in Ojang-dong.

The short version: **lunch at the old naengmyeon and ox-bone soup places around Euljiro 4-ga, evenings in the golbaengi (sea snail) alley and nogari alley at Euljiro 3-ga, and tea at Eulji Dabang, open since 1985.** The only MICHELIN Guide Seoul 2026 Bib Gourmand in this area is **Woo Lae Oak**. Redevelopment is still under way, though, and several famous names have closed or moved to other neighbourhoods. Every restaurant here was checked against Seoul city, KTO, MICHELIN, government data or news reports, and anything said about flavour comes only from those descriptions.

## What this guide covers

Here, Euljiro means **Euljiro 1-ga to 5-ga and the alleys on both sides of the main road, the Sewoon Sangga area, Bangsan and Jungbu markets, and the neighbourhoods just south (Jeo-dong, Cho-dong, Inhyeon-dong, Ojang-dong, Chungmuro 3-ga)**. The northern edge is Cheonggyecheon stream and the southern edge is roughly Toegye-ro.

The neighbours have their own guides: [Myeongdong](/en/posts/myeongdong-food-2026-09/) for Myeongdong Kyoja, Hadongkwan and Namdaemun Market; [Gwanghwamun and City Hall](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-food-2026-09/) for Da-dong's Bumin-ok and Nampo Myeonok; [Dongdaemun](/en/posts/dongdaemun-food-2026-10/) for Euljiro 6-ga eastwards, Gwanghui-dong and dakhanmari; and [Gwangjang Market](/en/posts/gwangjang-market-2026-09/) across the stream. This page sticks to what lies in between.

## The nogari alley: it started with one beer franchise in 1980

The **Euljiro nogari alley was designated Seoul Future Heritage (no. 2015-029) in 2015.** Seoul Future Heritage is a city programme that recognises places and businesses worth remembering; it is not a legal protection. The alley sits around 19 Eulji-ro 13-gil. The city's write-up gives this timeline:

| Year | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 1980 | Eulji OB Bear opens as part of the OB Bear draught beer chain |
| 1989 | The second pub, Munich Hof, opens |
| From 1997 | Customers surge after the Asian financial crisis |
| 2012 | The nogari pub merchants' association forms |
| 2015 | Seoul Future Heritage designation |

The founder of Eulji OB Bear, Kang Hyo-geun, **came from Hwanghae Province and missed the pollack he used to eat in winter kimchi at home**, so he put nogari on the menu, according to the city. In the early years **a 500 cc beer cost ₩380**, and with a ₩100 snack you paid under ₩500. Nogari is the standard Korean word for **young pollack**, dried and grilled as a drinking snack.

At the time of designation the city counted **about ten pubs**, and noted that once you sat down, **draught beer and nogari arrived for each person without ordering**. It also said the alley sold around 60 tonnes of beer a day, **40 tonnes of it at Manseon Hof**. Those details describe the alley around 2015 and may not match how it works today.

KTO explains the background this way: Euljiro was the country's largest printing district, many workers pulled all-nighters to meet deadlines, and pubs multiplied as they stopped for nogari and beer between shifts. At first the fish was **grilled over coal briquettes and served with gochujang**; today many pubs also serve fried chicken, stews and fried snacks. Hours **vary by pub** (KTO).

### Street tables: allowed in 2017, permits ended after 2022

The plastic tables spread across the road, known in Korean as *yajang*, are part of the alley's image, but the rules have changed several times.

- **May 2017**: Jung-gu district formally **allowed outdoor trading** in the alley, covering a 465 m stretch and **17 pubs** (Dong-A Ilbo, 11 May 2017).
- **October 2022**: the district was issuing **March-to-November road-use permits to 20 businesses for about ₩90 million a year**, but said it would stop from the following year because of redevelopment works nearby (B tv Seoul News, 25 October 2022).
- **April 2024**: OhmyNews reported that the district **cancelled the road-use permits in November 2022** and had been removing tables and issuing fines since. The evening car-free street (8-11 pm) **ended in July 2023**, a district official said.
- **October 2024**: Seoul city sent Jung-gu a notice titled "street vendor inspection results and request for action (Euljiro nogari alley)"; the content is not public.

Even so, the Hankook Ilbo reported in May 2024 that **many pubs still put tables outside** after the ban, and Manseon Hof told OhmyNews its tables were **on private land, not the road**. In August 2026 a Seoul city press release described an advisory committee touring the **"nogari yajang street"** and said the golbaengi and nogari alleys had created a **food scene and street-drinking culture** popular with young people and foreign visitors.

In short, **there may or may not be tables outside on the night you go.** Which pubs set out seating, and on what legal basis, could not be confirmed from official sources.

### Eulji OB Bear: eviction and afterwards

The alley's original pub left in 2022. According to Yonhap (21 April 2022):

- A dispute with the building owner began in **2018**; Eulji OB Bear lost the eviction case at two levels and the Supreme Court dismissed its appeal.
- In **January 2022** the owner of Manseon Hof bought part of the building.
- At **about 4:20 am on 21 April 2022**, the sixth attempt at enforcement removed the sign and all the furniture.
- Eulji OB Bear began as **the first franchise outlet recruited by Dongyang Beer, OB's predecessor**, and was registered as a "100-year shop" by the Ministry of SMEs and Startups.

The JoongAng Ilbo (15 February 2024) reported that Eulji OB Bear **reopened in March 2023 near the Gyeongui Line Book Street in Mapo-gu**, and would soft-open from 16 February 2024 **near Euljiro 3-ga Station exit 10**, across the main road from the old alley; the owner said street tables would not be possible there. A GQ Korea piece in June 2026 featured the Mapo branch. **Whether the Euljiro branch is still open could not be confirmed.**

### Manseon Hof and redevelopment

The same JoongAng piece said the space Eulji OB Bear left had been **filled with Manseon Hof branches**, and an OhmyNews reporter counted the word "Manseon" on **7 of the 14 beer pubs** in April 2024. Manseon's owner told the paper the branches had been handed to individual operators and three had closed.

Redevelopment is reshaping the area. Newsis (4 April 2024) reported that Seoul's urban planning committee approved a revised plan for the **Supyo redevelopment zone around 237 Ipjeong-dong**, where a **33-storey office tower** will go up. Manseon Hof is outside the zone, but **roughly half the old nogari pubs are expected to disappear**. An August 2026 analysis in Money S (Hankyung Magazine) grouped exits 3 and 4 of Euljiro 3-ga Station as the "Manseon Hof and nogari alley" zone and found **30.3% of card spending there came from overseas**, a sign of how many foreign visitors come.

## The golbaengi alley: an after-work snack since around 1970

Separate from the nogari alley, Euljiro 3-ga has a **golbaengi (sea snail) alley**. Seoul's 2021 list of nine Future Heritage food alleys includes the **"Euljiro golbaengi alley", formed in 1968**, and Jung-gu lists it among its **seven food streets**.

Jung-gu points visitors to **Euljiro 3-ga Station exit 12** and describes the dish: sea snails heaped with **shredded spring onion, chilli flakes and garlic**, with a **rolled omelette on the side**. A Yonhap column (13 July 2026) says the alley formed **around 1970** as industrialisation took off, frequented by tool-market and print workers and office staff after work. The same column notes that most canned golbaengi eaten in Korea are whelks from the North Atlantic.

Names and prices of individual shops in the alley could not be found in official sources, so none are listed.

## Pyongyang naengmyeon: Woo Lae Oak and Pyeongraeok

Naengmyeon is Korean cold buckwheat noodles. The Pyongyang style comes in a clear meat broth; the Hamheung style, covered further down, is usually eaten with a spicy sauce.

![A white bowl of cold noodles in clear broth on a wooden table, topped with sliced beef, half a boiled egg and pickled radish](/images/euljiro-food-2026-09/06.webp)

This 2019 photo on Wikimedia Commons is labelled "South Korean style Pyongyang naengmyeon"; the restaurant is not identified, and it is not from any place in this guide.

### Woo Lae Oak: from "Seobukgwan" in 1946

**Woo Lae Oak** (62-29 Changgyeonggung-ro) is a **Bib Gourmand** in the MICHELIN Guide Seoul 2026. Of Seoul's 51 Bib Gourmands, it is the only one inside this guide's area.

According to Seoul Future Heritage (2013-201), it began in **1946 as "Seobukgwan", opened by Jang Won-il near the Euljiro 4-ga rotary**, drawing on his experience running a restaurant in Pyongyang. After **fleeing during the Korean War, the family came back and renamed it "Woo Lae Oak", meaning "came back again"**. The present building dates from 1988, and the founder's granddaughter runs it in the third generation.

Two official descriptions of the broth:

- Seoul Future Heritage: Hanwoo beef rump and leg simmered whole for **4-5 hours**, with only buckwheat noodles added.
- MICHELIN: Hanwoo shank boiled for hours and **seasoned with nothing but salt and soy sauce**; it also recommends the **bulgogi** with the noodles.

KTO says Woo Lae Oak seasons its broth **a little more than other Pyongyang naengmyeon places, so first-timers find it easier**.

- Hours: **Tue-Sun 11:30-21:00, last order 20:20; closed Mondays and 1 January** (KTO, MICHELIN)
- Prices: **Pyongyang naengmyeon, bibim naengmyeon, onmyeon (warm noodles) and kimchi-mari naengmyeon ₩18,000 each (about $13.50)** (DiningCode listing)
- No reservations, cards accepted, valet parking (MICHELIN). About 100 m from Euljiro 4-ga Station exit 4 (KTO).

### Pyeongraeok: Myeongdong, Pimatgol, then Jeo-dong

![A wide bowl of chogyetang, icy broth heaped with lettuce, radish and shredded chicken, surrounded by small side dishes](/images/euljiro-food-2026-09/07.webp)

This is a generic chogyetang photo from Wikimedia Commons, not from Pyeongraeok.

**Pyeongraeok** (21-1 Mareunnae-ro, Jeo-dong 2-ga) was made **Seoul Future Heritage (2020-021) in 2020**. The city says **a man from Pyeongan Province opened it opposite the Jungang Theatre in Myeongdong in 1950**; it moved in 1967 and **closed in 2007 when Jongno's Pimatgol alleys were redeveloped**. After three years with only its phone line kept alive, it **reopened in Jeo-dong in 2010**.

KTO names **chogyetang** (chilled chicken broth with noodles and vegetables) and **mung bean pancakes** as its signatures, and says the **dak-muchim** (shredded chicken tossed with cucumber) also comes as a side dish. Hours are **11:00-22:00 (break 15:30-17:00, last order 21:00), open every day**, cards accepted (KTO). DiningCode lists **Pyongyang naengmyeon ₩14,000, yukgaejang ₩11,000, mung bean pancake ₩20,000 and dak-muchim ₩25,000**.

### Euljimyeonok has moved to Nagwon-dong

**Euljimyeonok**, once part of every Euljiro naengmyeon list, is no longer in this area. According to the Segye Ilbo (26 June 2022) and Money Today (22 April 2024), it spent 37 years in Ipjeong-dong from 1985, served its **last meal there on 25 June 2022** because of the **Sewoon 3-2 redevelopment zone**, and **reopened on 22 April 2024 in Nagwon-dong, Jongno-gu**, near Jongno 3-ga Station. Noodles were ₩15,000 at the reopening (2024 figure).

### Pildong Myeonok is just outside

MICHELIN 2026's other naengmyeon Bib Gourmand, **Pildong Myeonok** (26 Seoae-ro), is near Chungmuro Station and Namsangol Hanok Village, south of Toegye-ro and outside this guide. It is noted here only because many people visit both.

## Hamheung naengmyeon: Ojang-dong's noodle street

![Bibim naengmyeon in a steel bowl, thin noodles under a mound of red chilli sauce and chopped spring onion](/images/euljiro-food-2026-09/01.webp)

This is a generic bibim naengmyeon photo from Wikimedia Commons, not from an Ojang-dong restaurant.

South of Euljiro 4-ga, **Ojang-dong** is the home of **Hamheung naengmyeon**. Jung-gu lists the **"Ojang-dong naengmyeon street"** as a food street and says that **starting with Heungnamjip in 1952, it grew to six restaurants in the 1970s, but only Heungnamjip, Ojang Naengmyeon and Sinchang Myeonok remain**. Directions: **400 m straight from exit 6 of Dongdaemun History & Culture Park Station**.

Two of them are Seoul Future Heritage:

| Restaurant | Address | Heritage no. | History (Seoul Future Heritage) | DiningCode listing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ojang-dong Heungnamjip | 114 Mareunnae-ro | 2020-013 | Opened 1953 by No Yong-eon; daughter-in-law took over in 1968; third generation | Mixed, raw-fish bibim and mul-naengmyeon ₩15,000 each; 11:00-20:30 |
| Ojang-dong Hamheung Naengmyeon | 108 Mareunnae-ro | 2013-193 | Founded by a refugee from the North after the Korean War; third generation | Hoe-, mul- and bibim naengmyeon ₩15,000 each; 11:00-20:00 (break 15:30-17:00) |

The heritage page for **Ojang-dong Hamheung Naengmyeon** says Hamheung naengmyeon is **"a dish that does not actually exist in Hamheung"**: the founder, who came from Heungnam, made Hamheung-style **"nongma guksu"** (potato-starch noodles) and the name stuck. Customers called it "the place in Ojang-dong that does Hamheung naengmyeon", which became its name. It was also chosen as a **2024 Seoul Oraegage**, the city's label for shops trading for 30 years or more.

The founding year is given as both **1953 (main text) and 1958 (timeline)** on the same heritage page, and Jung-gu dates Heungnamjip to 1952 while the heritage page says 1953; both are shown here with their sources.

The third restaurant Jung-gu names, **Sinchang Myeonok**, could not be found with an address or hours in official sources.

## Soups and grills: Munhwaok, Sunheungok, Sansugapsan, Joseonok

![Milky seolleongtang in a black stone pot, with noodles and beef under sliced spring onion](/images/euljiro-food-2026-09/08.webp)

This is a generic seolleongtang photo from Wikimedia Commons, not from Munhwaok.

**Munhwaok** (62-5 Changgyeonggung-ro), in the lane next to Woo Lae Oak, serves seolleongtang, a milky ox-bone soup. Seoul Future Heritage (2013-100) says it **opened in Jongno 5-ga in 1952 and moved here in 1957**, and is run by the founder's daughter-in-law with her daughter. The city even gives directions: from **Euljiro 4-ga Station exit 4**, look for a **yellow sign with red letters reading 문화옥** and follow the lane; the restaurant is on the left. The menu centres on brisket and ox-head seolleongtang, with doganitang, oxtail soup, trotter soup and boiled meats. DiningCode lists **seolleongtang ₩15,000 (large ₩18,000) and oxtail soup ₩26,000**, open **07:00-21:00 (break 15:00-17:00)**, an early start for the area.

**Sunheungok** (9 Eulji-ro 33-gil) serves **only two dishes: braised oxtail and oxtail soup** (Seoul Future Heritage 2020-010). The name means "may it prosper smoothly". Its braised oxtail, three pieces with potato under a heap of blanched spring onion, earned the nickname **"spring-onion-smothered oxtail"**, and the custom of adding thin noodles was introduced by the second-generation owner, the city says. The same page gives the founding year as both **1945 and 1949**. Current hours and prices could not be confirmed.

**Sansugapsan** (24 Eulji-ro 20-gil, Inhyeon-dong 1-ga) is a **sundae-gukbap** (blood-sausage soup with rice) restaurant listed by KTO, about 5 minutes on foot from Euljiro 4-ga Station exit 10. Hours are **11:30-21:30 (break 15:00-17:00, last orders 14:30 and 20:30), closed Sundays** (KTO). DiningCode lists **sundae-gukbap ₩10,000 (large ₩12,000) and the sundae set meal ₩15,000**, and the menu notes that **the set is served for one only at lunch (11:00-14:30); otherwise groups order from two portions**.

**Joseonok** (6-5 Eulji-ro 15-gil, Euljiro 3-ga) is introduced by KTO as a **Hanwoo galbi restaurant over 50 years old**. Its signature is marinated beef short ribs, with galbitang, daegutang (made with Hanwoo and Korean beef, per KTO), jangguk-bap and naengmyeon. KTO lists **11:30-21:30 (break 14:30-16:30), closed Sundays**; DiningCode lists **marinated beef ribs (250 g) ₩43,000, galbitang ₩15,000 and jangguk-bap ₩13,000**, and gives closing as 21:00.

## Chinese and Italian: Andongjang and La Cantina

**Andongjang** (124 Eulji-ro) is described in its Seoul Future Heritage entry (2013-175) as **"the oldest Chinese restaurant in Seoul"**. It opened around 1948 near today's Piccadilly cinema, moved here in 1950 and registered as a business in 1964; the founder's grandson runs it now. "Andong" comes from a **place name in Shandong, China**. The city names **oyster jjamppong with garlic** and **menbosha** (fried prawn toast) as its signatures.

- Hours: **11:30-21:00, open every day** (KTO). One minute from Euljiro 3-ga Station exit 10.
- Prices: **oyster jjamppong ₩12,000, samseon udon ₩11,000, japtang-bap ₩16,000** (DiningCode listing)

**La Cantina** (basement, Samsung Building, 19 Eulji-ro) is Seoul Future Heritage (2013-084) and a **2024 Seoul Oraegage**. It opened around 1967 as an Italian restaurant; in the early days ingredients and chefs were hard to find, and the Italian ambassador's wife taught recipes, according to the city. It was once a popular spot for matchmaking meetings and even lent jackets to diners. KTO lists **11:30-22:00 (break 15:00-17:00), closed Sundays and at lunch on public holidays**.

## Tea rooms and cafés: Eulji Dabang and Coffee Hanyakbang

**Eulji Dabang** is a *dabang*, the old-style Korean tea room, opened in 1985 **on the second floor of the Euljimyeonok building**. An Asia Economy interview (27 March 2024) says the Sewoon 3-2 redevelopment forced it to close for about two months before it **moved across the road towards Euljiro 3-ga Station exit 10**. KTO now gives the address as **2F, 124-1 Eulji-ro**.

KTO names **ssanghwa-cha with an egg yolk floating on top** as the signature, and says the place became a stop for fans after BTS used it for a 2021 season's greetings shoot. Hours are **Mon-Sat 06:00-21:00, Sun 09:00-20:00, open every day** (KTO). DiningCode lists **ssanghwa-cha, ginseng, jujube and ginger tea at ₩6,000 each**. DiningCode still shows an older address (31 Eulji-ro 33-gil); this guide follows KTO, updated April 2026.

**Coffee Hanyakbang** (16-6 Samil-daero 12-gil, Euljiro 2-ga) is a café that KTO says occupies the **site of Hyeminseo, a Joseon-era public clinic**. It is tucked inside a narrow alley and can be hard to find. Ground floor **10:00-22:00 weekdays, 11:00-22:00 weekends (last order 21:00)**, upper floors 10:00-21:30, open every day (KTO).

## Markets: Bangsan and Jungbu

**Bangsan Market** (around 18-1 Eulji-ro 33-gil) was long known for baking and packaging supplies, but a Korea Economic Daily report (13 February 2026) found big changes. Traders said customers moved online after COVID-19; a 45 m lane that used to be 80% baking suppliers now has **more packaging shops**, and **only four baking-supply shops** remain in the whole market. Food stalls inside the market could not be confirmed from official sources.

**Jungbu Market** (35 Eulji-ro 36-gil, Ojang-dong) is a **wholesale market for dried seafood, fruit and produce** according to KTO, selling laver, seaweed, anchovies, squid and dried pollack. A 2019 Mediafine article on Seoul Future Heritage says Jungbu and Sin-Jungbu markets were **designated Seoul Future Heritage in 2013**, that **nogari** is among the main goods, and that it then opened **3 am-6 pm on weekdays and closed Sundays** (2019 figures). KTO gives the opening date as February 1957, while the article dates the opening ceremony to February 1959.

## Good to know

**Card payment cannot legally be refused.** Article 19 of the Specialized Credit Finance Business Act says **card merchants may not refuse card payment or treat card users less favourably**. Official sources list card payment at Woo Lae Oak (MICHELIN, KTO) and Pyeongraeok (KTO). The law does not guarantee every terminal accepts foreign cards, so carry a little cash or a Korean payment method in markets and pub alleys.

**Watch Sundays and Mondays.** Sansugapsan and Joseonok close on Sundays, Woo Lae Oak on Mondays. Andongjang and Pyeongraeok open every day (KTO).

**Some dishes come in two portions minimum.** Sansugapsan's sundae set is single-portion only at lunch (DiningCode listing). If Korean food is new to you, [Korean food for beginners](/en/posts/korean-food-for-beginners-2026-09/) is a good start.

**Sources disagree on details.** Joseonok's closing time (KTO 21:30, DiningCode 21:00) and Eulji Dabang's address (KTO 124-1 Eulji-ro, DiningCode 31 Eulji-ro 33-gil) differ. Check a map app before setting out.

**No tipping.** The menu price is what you pay.

- **Nogari alley**: started with Eulji OB Bear in 1980; Seoul Future Heritage in 2015. Street trading allowed in 2017, road-use permits ended after 2022, and Eulji OB Bear was evicted in April 2022. Redevelopment will change about half the alley.
