Walk from Gwanghwamun Gate down to Jonggak and you cross four public squares: **Gwanghwamun Square, Seoul Plaza, Cheonggye Plaza and the forecourt of Bosingak**.

They have one thing in common. **All four sit on space that the city took back from cars between 2004 and 2023.** Gwanghwamun Square was carved out of the middle of a 16-lane road. Seoul Plaza was a traffic roundabout. Cheonggyecheon stream ran under an elevated expressway. And the stone terrace in front of Gwanghwamun Gate, the Woldae, was torn up for tram tracks in 1923 and only came back a century later.

The palaces and Bukchon are covered in our [Jongno guide](/en/posts/jongno-guide-2026-09/). This guide looks south of the palace, at **the squares along Sejong-daero, read through official records**.

![Gwanghwamun Gate and the Gyeongbokgung palace wall under a blue sky with white clouds, a person in red sitting on the lawn in front, visitors lined up along the stone railing before the gate](/images/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/04.webp)

## The four squares at a glance

| Square | What was there before | Now | District |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Gwanghwamun Square** | 16-lane Sejong-ro | Opened 2009, reopened 2022 after absorbing the western carriageway | Jongno-gu |
| **Seoul Plaza** | Traffic circle around a fountain outside City Hall | Lawn plaza since 1 May 2004 | Jung-gu |
| **Cheonggye Plaza / Cheonggyecheon** | Covered stream under an elevated road | Restored 1 October 2005 | Jung-gu / Jongno-gu boundary |
| **Bosingak area** | Joseon-era bell pavilion site, redevelopment land | Current pavilion 1979, underground museum 2018 | Jongno-gu |

The addresses split between two districts (gu). The Korea Tourism Organization (KTO) lists Gwanghwamun Square at **172 Sejong-daero, Jongno-gu**; the city lists Seoul Plaza at **110 Sejong-daero, Jung-gu**; Cheonggye Plaza is in **Taepyeongno 1-ga, Jung-gu**; and the Bosingak site is at **54 Jong-ro, Jongno-gu**. Two squares on the same boulevard answer to different district offices.

## Gwanghwamun Square: from 16 lanes to a 60-metre square

### 2009: an island in the traffic

According to the city's own history of the square, Sejong-daero was widened from the 1960s onward and became **a space built around cars**. After Seoul Plaza (2004) and Namdaemun Plaza (2005), the city drew up plans for Gwanghwamun in 2006.

- **1 August 2009**: the square opens, **34m wide, 557m long, 18,014㎡**
- The road shrinks from **16 lanes to 10**
- **9 October 2009** (Hangeul Day): the King Sejong statue is unveiled

The Admiral Yi Sun-sin statue at the south end is much older, erected on **27 April 1968**.

The 2009 square was **an island with traffic on both sides**. To reach it you crossed a road.

### 2021-2022: the western carriageway becomes square

The second round of work began on **16 November 2020**, and the order of events is worth knowing.

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 16 November 2020 | Restructuring works begin |
| **00:00, 6 March 2021** | The east-side road (previously five lanes) is widened to 7-9 lanes and becomes **two-way**. Traffic stops on the west-side road in front of the Sejong Center |
| May 2021 | Sejong-daero People's Forest Road completed |
| **6 August 2022** | Gwanghwamun Square reopens |

The city's July 2022 press release gave these figures:

| Item | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area | 18,840㎡ | **40,300㎡ (2.1×)** |
| Width | 35m | **60m** |
| Green space | 2,830㎡ | **9,367㎡ (3.3×)** |
| Trees planted | - | About 5,000 |

With the whole west carriageway turned into square, the plaza now runs right up to the Sejong Center side. The same release describes a **212-metre "history water path"** engraved with a timeline from 1392 to 2022, and remains of the **Saheonbu (Office of the Inspector-General) gate** displayed where they were found during the works.

### 2023: the Woldae

The broad raised terrace directly in front of Gwanghwamun Gate is the **Woldae**. According to Kyunghyang Shinmun (12 October 2023), the excavated terrace measures about **48.7m north-south by 29.7m east-west**, with a central royal path about 7m wide leading to the gate's middle arch.

- **1923**: demolished when the Japanese colonial authorities laid tram lines
- **15 October 2023**: restoration ceremony, closing a "restore Gwanghwamun" project that began in 2006
- The same day a **new name board** went up. The 2010 board had black characters on white; the new one has **gold characters on black**
- A pair of carved stone beasts for the balustrade ends were donated in August 2023 by the family of the late Samsung chairman Lee Kun-hee

### 2026: the Garden of Gratitude

On **12 May 2026** the **Garden of Gratitude** opened on the square to honour the countries that fought in the Korean War.

- Above ground: **23 stone columns, each 6.25m tall** ("Light of Gratitude 23"), for Korea and 22 allied nations
- Below ground: a media exhibition space, "Freedom Hall", **Tue-Sun 10:00-20:00, closed Mondays**
- Night lighting: daily 20:00-23:00

YTN reported that some civic groups and ruling-party politicians **criticised the design as out of keeping with the square's other monuments**. A Money Today report from August 2026 said the square drew 1,347,350 visitors in the ten days after the opening (13-23 May), and about **27 million visitors in 2025, or 74,000 a day**.

## Seoul Plaza: a lawn built in 79 days

![The striped lawn of Seoul Plaza, with the old stone City Hall building and its clock tower in front of the curving glass facade of the new City Hall](/images/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/05.webp)

The Seoul Plaza website titles its construction page **"a record of 79 days"**.

| Item | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Before | A traffic circle around a fountain completed on **29 June 1963**, for about 40 years |
| Accidents in 2001 | **70, with 67 injured**. Second-worst accident spot |
| August 2002 citizen poll | 79% for, 15% against (1,000 people) |
| Work began | 19 March 2004 |
| Opened | **1 May 2004** |
| Area | About 13,207㎡ in total, 6,449㎡ lawn (Korean Wikipedia) |

The stone building behind the lawn was built in **1926 as the Gyeongseong (colonial-era Seoul) city hall**. The Korea Heritage Portal lists it as registered heritage; it served as Seoul City Hall after liberation and reopened as **Seoul Library** on 26 October 2012. The glass building behind it is today's City Hall.

### Seoul Plaza's two sizes

A 2021 press release on the Sejong-daero People's Forest Road said the 13,950㎡ of new pedestrian space was **"more than twice the size of Seoul Plaza (6,449㎡)"**. That 6,449㎡ matches what Korean Wikipedia gives as the **lawn** alone. Measured as a whole (13,207㎡), the plaza is about the same size as the new pavement. I could not find the total on an official city page, so both figures are given here with their sources.

### Sejong-daero People's Forest Road

South of Seoul Plaza runs the **Sejong-daero People's Forest Road**, completed in 2021. City figures:

- Route: Sejong-daero intersection - City Hall - Sungnyemun Gate - Seoul Station, **1.55km**
- Lanes: **9-12 reduced to 7-9**
- 13,950㎡ of new pedestrian space, pavements up to 12m wide
- 418 trees, 15,000 shrubs, 130,000 flowering plants
- Average traffic speed before and after: 21.7km/h and 21.6km/h

The city says that together with the Gwanghwamun west-side conversion this creates a **2.6km walking axis from Gwanghwamun to Seoul Station**.

## Two squares, two opposite rulebooks

This was the most surprising find. Seoul Plaza and Gwanghwamun Square sit on the same boulevard, but **their ordinances point in opposite directions**.

| Item | Seoul Plaza | Gwanghwamun Square |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Article 1 purpose | Leisure and cultural activities, public-interest events and **the holding of assemblies and demonstrations** | Citizens' healthy **leisure and cultural activities** |
| Procedure | **Notification** (90 to 5 days ahead) | **Permit application** (60 to 7 days ahead) |
| The city's role | "Shall in principle accept" the notification | "Shall review" whether use fits the square's purpose "and decide whether to permit" |

Seoul Plaza was not always like this. Seoul Shinmun reported that in **December 2009 a citizen-initiated petition with about 100,000 signatures** asked for the ordinance to change, and Hankook Ilbo reported that on **13 August 2010 the Seoul Metropolitan Council passed an amendment replacing the permit system with notification**. The words "assemblies and demonstrations" in Article 1 carry an amendment date of 27 September 2010.

Gwanghwamun Square went the other way. Two days before the 2022 reopening, **on 4 August 2022, the city stated** that the square is permitted for leisure and cultural use and that **rallies and demonstrations are in principle not eligible for a permit**. A five-member advisory panel (noise, events, law, traffic) reviews large events.

But **a permit for the square and a rally on the road are separate matters.** Rallies are frequently held on Sejong-daero right next to the square, and the road is closed when they are.

## Cheonggyecheon: a stream where an expressway stood

![People walking past 'Spring', a red and blue striped spiral sculpture at Cheonggye Plaza, with office towers lining Cheonggyecheon-ro into the distance](/images/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/13.webp)

From the city's article marking 20 years since restoration (October 2025):

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 1958 | Covering of the stream begins |
| 1967 | Cheonggye Elevated Road construction begins |
| **1 July 2003** | Overpass demolition and restoration works begin |
| **1 October 2005** | Restoration completed, a **5.8km** stream through downtown |
| 2015 | 190 million cumulative visitors |
| 2025 | About **330 million** cumulative visitors (estimate) |

The stream begins at **Cheonggye Plaza**. The spiral in the photo is **"Spring"** by Claes Oldenburg and Coosje van Bruggen; Segye Ilbo reported in July 2017 that it was repainted **for the first time in 11 years**. Follow the stream east and you reach **HiKR Ground**, the KTO's visitor centre ([our HiKR Ground guide](/en/posts/hikr-ground-pikachu-2026-09/)), which the same city article says opened in July 2022 and passed 2 million visitors by March 2025.

## Jonggak: a new bell, and an old street underground

![Front view of Bosingak, a two-storey pavilion with red pillars and green painted eaves, the name board '普信閣' under the roof and a stone balustrade and steps in front](/images/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/10.webp)

### Bosingak

From the KTO's description of the Bosingak site:

- **1395**: the first bell pavilion is built. It burned four times and was rebuilt eight times
- **1895**: King Gojong grants the name "Bosingak". It is also called "Jonggak", literally "bell pavilion", which is where the station name comes from
- **The current building dates from August 1979**, a two-storey pavilion built by the city
- In the Joseon era the bell rang 33 times at 4am and 28 times at 7pm to open and close the city gates

**The bell hanging there now is not the 1468 original.** The National Museum of Korea says the old bell developed cracks and was used for the New Year's Eve ringing **only until 1985**; it was moved to the museum in **December 2004**. The current bell was cast with public donations, hung on 14 August 1985 and first rung the next day, Liberation Day.

### New Year's Eve and road closures

Seoul's guide to the 2025 ceremony:

- 22:50 on 31 December 2025 to 00:20 on 1 January 2026, around Bosingak, **33 strikes of the bell**
- Full road closure 18:00 on 31 December to 07:00 on 1 January: **Jong-ro (Sejong-daero to Jongno 2-ga), Cheonggyebuk-ro and Ujeongguk-ro**
- Last trains on Seoul Metro lines 1-9 and others **extended to 2am**, plus 45 bus routes nearby

If this year's closures are similar, getting a taxi to a hotel near Jonggak on the evening of 31 December will be difficult. The 2026 ceremony details had not been published at the time of writing.

### Gongpyeong Historic Site Museum

In the basement of an office tower east of Bosingak is the **Gongpyeong Historic Site Museum**. According to the Seoul Museum of History (12 September 2018), it preserves **building sites and lanes excavated during the 2015 redevelopment of Gongpyeong blocks 1, 2 and 4, in the exact place they were found**.

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Opened | 12 September 2018 |
| Size | 3,817㎡ floor area (described as Seoul's largest archaeological site museum) |
| What you see | 16th- to 17th-century building sites, **42m of Joseon-era lane you can actually walk on**, three reconstructed houses, around 1,000 everyday objects |
| Hours | 09:00-18:00 (last entry 17:30), **free** |
| Closed | Mondays and 1 January, per the 2018 opening release |
| Getting there | [[line:1]] Jonggak Station exit 3-1 |

The tower went up, but **the Joseon lane underneath was kept in its basement instead of being cleared**. The current visitor page does not list closing days, so check before you go.

## What I got wrong

**One: Gwanghwamun Square was not built once.** The 2009 and 2022 squares differ in size by more than double. The first was an island between carriageways; the current one swallowed the entire western carriageway.

**Two: the 2009 square's size depends on the source.** The city's 2025 history gives **18,014㎡ and 34m wide**; the 2022 reopening release gives the "previous" square as **18,840㎡ and 35m wide**. I could not establish which is right or whether they measure differently.

**Three: I assumed both squares had the same rules.** Seoul Plaza's ordinance is a **notification system that names assemblies and demonstrations as a purpose**; Gwanghwamun Square's is a **permit system for leisure and culture**.

**Four: I assumed the bell at Bosingak was the Joseon original.** A new bell has been rung since Liberation Day 1985; the 1468 bell is at the National Museum of Korea.

Yeonnam buried a railway to make a park; this part of Seoul **turned the road itself into public space**.

### Check for weekend rallies

Road closures are common here on weekends and public holidays. Two examples reported in 2026:

- **4 April (Easter weekend)**: Sejong-daero from Gwanghwamun intersection to Sejong intersection **closed 00:00-24:00**, with closures also on Jong-ro, Eulji-ro, Sajik-ro and Yulgok-ro (Seoul Shinmun)
- **15 August (Liberation Day)**: closures around Sejong-ro and from Euljiro 1-ga towards City Hall, with about 218 traffic police deployed (Newspim)

Both times police urged people to **use public transport, especially the subway**. Seoul Shinmun reported that closure information is available from the Seoul Metropolitan Police traffic information line, the city's **TOPIS** traffic website and a KakaoTalk traffic information service. Buses and taxis get stuck, but the subway runs, so on weekends plan your route around stations.

## How to spend the day

Walk north to south, then east: **Gwanghwamun → City Hall → Cheonggye Plaza → Jonggak**, and you pass all four squares.

1. **Morning**: [[line:5]] Gwanghwamun Station for the square and the Woldae. Palace tips are in our [Jongno things to do](/en/posts/jongno-things-to-do-2026-09/); sights for this area alone are in [Gwanghwamun, City Hall and Jonggak things to do](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-things-to-do-2026-09/).
2. **Lunch**: see [Gwanghwamun, City Hall and Jonggak food](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-food-2026-09/).
3. **Afternoon**: down Sejong-daero to Seoul Plaza and Seoul Library, then from Cheonggye Plaza down to the stream.
4. **Late afternoon**: walk east along the stream and come up near Jonggak for Bosingak and the Gongpyeong museum. Last entry is 17:30.

Keep following the stream east and you reach Euljiro and Gwangjang Market; our [Euljiro guide](/en/posts/euljiro-guide-2026-09/) picks up from there.

## Where to stay

Gwanghwamun and City Hall are a government and office district. When choosing a hotel, bear in mind that weekend rallies and the New Year's Eve closure can **make it hard to reach the door by taxi**. Check on a map whether you can walk from the station. Comparisons are in [Gwanghwamun, City Hall and Jonggak hotels](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-hotels-2026-09/).

## Who this area suits

**Good fit:**

- You want to walk. Widened pavements run from Gwanghwamun to Seoul Station
- You want to see **how central Seoul has changed**, from a Joseon palace gate to a 1920s city hall to 2020s squares, in one walk
- You want free sights. The squares, the stream, Bosingak and the Gongpyeong museum cost nothing

**Not a good fit:**

- For quiet lanes and hanok houses, try our [Bukchon guide](/en/posts/bukchon-guide-2026-09/) or [Seochon guide](/en/posts/seochon-guide-2026-09/)
- If you plan to drive on a weekend, check rally closures first

## Summary

- All four squares between Gwanghwamun and Jonggak **sit on former roads or an expressway**.
- **Gwanghwamun Square** opened on 1 August 2009 after cutting 16 lanes to 10, and **reopened on 6 August 2022 at 40,300㎡ (2.1×) after absorbing the western carriageway**.
- The **Woldae**, destroyed for tram tracks in 1923, returned on **15 October 2023** along with a gold-on-black name board.
- **Seoul Plaza** replaced a fountain roundabout **after 79 days of work, opening on 1 May 2004**.
- **The two squares follow different ordinances**: Seoul Plaza uses **notification** and names demonstrations as a purpose; Gwanghwamun Square uses **permits** for leisure and culture.
- **Cheonggyecheon**: overpass demolition from 1 July 2003, **5.8km restored on 1 October 2005**, about 330 million visitors in 20 years.
- **Bosingak**: the current bell dates from 1985; the 1468 bell is at the National Museum of Korea. Next door, the free **Gongpyeong Historic Site Museum** keeps 42m of Joseon lane underground.
- [[line:5]] Gwanghwamun, [[line:1]] [[line:2]] City Hall, [[line:1]] Jonggak. On weekends, check for rallies and travel by subway.

In one line: **the walk from Gwanghwamun to Jonggak is twenty years of Seoul handing traffic lanes back to people, one square at a time, and each square plays by its own rules.**
