On 9 September 2026, the Hankyoreh newspaper reported building work all over Tongui-dong, the lane that runs along the west wall of Gyeongbokgung Palace. The sites included a bookshop run by Nobel laureate Han Kang (closed in July), a gallery cafe that had been there 14 years (closed in March) and a restaurant nearly 50 years old (closed in April). Nine months earlier, on 29 December 2025, South Korea's president had gone back to work at Cheong Wa Dae, the old presidential compound on Seochon's northern edge, for the first time in 1,330 days (KBS). Seochon is now living through **a second wave of gentrification and the return of the presidency at the same time.**

The short version: Seochon is **the "upper village" where Joseon-era clerks, interpreters and painters lived, kept low by nearly fifty years of height and development limits, and then made expensive twice by exactly that low skyline.** This is the overview, rebuilt from the original planning notices and dated news reports. Museums, the valley and the walking trails are in [Seochon: things to do](/en/posts/seochon-things-to-do-2026-09/), Tongin Market and restaurants in [Seochon food](/en/posts/seochon-food-2026-09/), and places to stay in [Seochon hotels](/en/posts/seochon-hotels-2026-09/).

![A Seochon hanok lane on a clear summer day. Hanok with patterned walls and wooden lattice windows line the street, with pots under the eaves, a red-brick wall and green trees on the right and white clouds overhead](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/22.webp)

Hanok (traditional Korean houses) next to a three-storey brick block is Seochon's default view. The Seoul Hanok Portal describes the area as a mix of "urban hanok, modern houses and contemporary multi-unit housing" from several eras.

## Seochon at a glance

The neighbourhood has several names, and each covers a different area.

| Name | What it refers to | Figures | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Seochon** ("west village") | The everyday name for the area between Gyeongbokgung and Inwangsan mountain. Korean Wikipedia bounds it by the palace (east), Inwangsan (west), the road in front of Sajikdan altar (south) and Changuimun gate and Bugaksan (north) | No official boundary | Korean Wikipedia |
| **Utdae / Udae / Sangdae village** | Joseon-era names meaning roughly "the upper village", because it sat at the head of the Cheonggyecheon stream inside the city wall | — | Sejong Village board, Wikipedia |
| **Sejong Village** | A name residents adopted from 2010 | — | Sejong Village board |
| **Gyeongbokgung West district-unit plan area** | The administrative name. Search planning documents under this name (경복궁서측) | **582,297 sq m**, "Chebu-dong and 14 other dong" | Seoul notices 2016-201 and 2020-179 |
| **Gyeongbokgung West (Seoul Hanok Portal)** | 14 legal neighbourhoods (dong) including Hyoja-dong and Ogin-dong | **About a third** of Bukchon | Seoul Hanok Portal |

582,297 square metres is a little more than 80 football pitches. Note the small mismatch: the city's planning notice says "Chebu-dong and 14 other dong" (15 in all), while the Hanok Portal says 14. Our [Jongno hotels](/en/posts/jongno-hotels-2026-09/) guide counted 14: Cheongun, Singyo, Gungjeong, Hyoja, Changseong, Tongui, Tongin, Nusang, Nuha, Ogin, Chebu, Pirun, Naeja and Sajik-dong.

## A place with many names: Utdae, Jangdong, Sejong Village

![The Korean text of the Sejong Village information board, explaining that the area was called Junsubang, Indalbang, Sunhwabang, Utdae, Udae and Sangdae village in the Joseon era and that residents began calling it Sejong Village in 2010](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/02.webp)

This is the first paragraph of the village information board. It lists Joseon administrative wards (Junsubang, Indalbang, Sunhwabang) alongside everyday names (Utdae, Udae, Sangdae village). Korean Wikipedia adds **Jangui-dong, Jangdong and Changui-dong**, names taken from Janguisa, a temple near Segeomjeong; the "Jangdong" in painter Jeong Seon's album *Eight Views of Jangdong* is this neighbourhood. "Seochon" itself comes from Inwangsan being the "western mountain", though Wikipedia notes it was rarely used in Joseon records.

The board's last paragraph reads, in translation:

> From 2010, residents began calling it Sejong Village, in the hope of making it a village of culture and the arts where the spirit of King Sejong lives on.

So **the name was chosen by residents, not by the city.** It rests on one small stone.

### The "Birthplace of King Sejong" stone

The Seoul Hanok Portal lists **"Birthplace of King Sejong (marker stone)"** among the early-Joseon traces of the area, alongside Sajikdan and Suseongdong Valley. King Sejong (born 1397) is the ruler credited with creating the Korean alphabet, Hangeul. According to a May 2026 Daum News report, the stone stands **by the road outside an optician's in Tongin-dong**, on the lane from Gyeongbokgung Station towards Tongin Market, and carries a single line: "Where King Sejong was born."

Two arguments surfaced in 2026:

- **Where exactly?** A civic group dedicated to restoring the birthplace compared about 25 sources, including old maps, colonial land surveys and a 1921 aerial photograph, and argued at a seminar on 11 May 2026 that the real site is **89-7 Tongin-dong, about 100 metres from the stone at 119-1**. There has been no official verification.
- **Restoration.** A Jongno district councillor called in December 2025 for excavation and preservation work, and academics and civic groups want a "national symbolic space".

The practical takeaway: **the stone is small and easy to miss, and there is no house or exhibition to visit.**

## A village of clerks, poets and painters

The Seoul Hanok Portal draws the contrast with Bukchon like this (translated):

> Unlike Bukchon, home to high officials and the scholar-gentry in the Joseon period, Gyeongbokgung West was mainly inhabited by the jungin class. With fine scenery beneath Inwangsan, it became in the late Joseon period the centre of wihang literature, where its writers enjoyed a culture of refined leisure.

The **jungin** ("middle people") were Joseon's hereditary technical class: interpreters, doctors, accountants, clerks. **Wihang literature** was the poetry and anthologies they produced outside the aristocratic establishment. The portal says the area was home to royals and powerful families in early Joseon, that **Ongnyu-dong** in today's Ogin-dong was the birthplace of wihang literature, and that the powerful Jangdong Kim clan and the artists around them, including **Jeong Seon and Kim Jeong-hui**, worked here. The village board credits the neighbourhood with Jeong Seon's famous *Clearing after Rain on Mount Inwang*.

Korean Wikipedia's entry on **Songseogwon** pins down the stage. Around **47 Ogin-dong**, the jungin poet Cheon Su-gyeong built a house called Songseogwon, and the gatherings held there, the **Songseogwon poetry society**, gave the whole area its name.

### Modern writers and artists

The Hanok Portal lists people who lived here from the colonial period on: **the poets Yi Sang, Yun Dong-ju and No Cheon-myeong, the painters Lee Yeo-seong, Lee Qoede and Lee Jung-seob, and later Park No-soo, Yi Sang-beom and Chun Kyung-ja.** Protected sites include **Park No-soo's house** (now the district-run Park No-soo Art Museum) and **Yi Sang-beom's house and studio** in Nuha-dong. Museum hours are in [Seochon: things to do](/en/posts/seochon-things-to-do-2026-09/).

### Byeoksusanjang: a French-style mansion on the poets' ground

![A black-and-white photograph from 1926 of the main hall of Byeoksusanjang, a Western-style stone mansion with a pointed tower, steep roofs and several storeys of windows, set against a rocky mountain](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/62.webp)

This 1926 photograph shows **Byeoksusanjang**. The Hanok Portal notes that during Japanese rule Seochon gained "official residences of the Government-General and the mansions of pro-Japanese figures such as Byeoksusanjang". According to Korean Wikipedia, Songseogwon passed through several owners before **Yun Deok-yeong**, an aristocrat who collaborated with the colonial regime, acquired it **around 1910**. He bought more than half of Ogin-dong and built this mansion around a French-style Western hall. After serving as offices for an international commission around the Korean War, the hall **burned in 1966 and was demolished in 1973**.

Traces remain. According to the city's news site (February 2024), the house Yun gave his daughter is today's **Park No-soo Art Museum**, and the house he built for his concubine is the **Ogin-dong Yun family house**. The city announced in February 2024 that the latter would be remodelled as a public space and opened in the first half of 2025; we could not confirm whether it has opened.

## Why it is low: half a century of height limits

Seochon's alleys are low because of **numbers in planning tables**, not taste. Here is the height-zone table from the city's 2016 notice.

## Redevelopment came, and was cancelled

"It was never possible to redevelop" is only half true. Seochon had several redevelopment zones; the city lifted most of them on its own authority between 2015 and 2017.

## What the district plan says: why the alleys are narrow and low

![A narrow Seochon hanok alley barely wide enough for two people side by side, lined with wooden gates and grey brick walls, gas meters and pipes on the walls and power lines overhead](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/24.webp)

Most Seochon alleys are about this wide. The rules that freeze these lanes and tiny plots are the **Gyeongbokgung West district-unit plan**, a Korean planning tool that sets detailed rules for a defined area.

### The notices

| Notice | Date | What it did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Seoul notice 2010-133** | **15 April 2010** | First designation of the district-unit plan area |
| Seoul notice 2011-31 | 17 January 2011 | Amendment |
| **Seoul notice 2016-201** | **14 July 2016** | Created a **563,151.2 sq m** residential-environment management area and reset plot sizes, floors and uses |
| Seoul notice 2020-179 | 1 May 2020 | Public-building designation for a new **Changseong-dong government annex**: 3 floors (12m), up to 4 floors (16m) in return for public benefits, with a history exhibition hall on the ground floor |

The 2020 notice cites the 2016 notice as dated "2016.7.4", but the original 2016 document is dated **14 July 2016**. We follow the original.

### Floors by zone (2016 notice)

| Zone | Maximum | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Designated hanok zone** | **1 floor** | A two-storey hanok may be allowed by the district building committee |
| Recommended hanok zone 1 | 2 floors | Plots next to designated hanok or old lanes under 4m wide |
| Recommended hanok zone 2 | 3 floors | 4 if conditions are met |
| General management, Pirundae-ro, waterway zones | 3 floors | East of Jahamun-ro, 4 floors if a recommended use is brought in |
| **Jahamun-ro and Hyoja-ro zones** | **4 floors** | Jahamun-ro up to 5 if conditions are met |
| Sajik-ro zone | 30m | Or the height-zone limit (15, 16 or 18m) where one applies |

That is why the main roads (Jahamun-ro, Hyoja-ro) carry four- and five-storey buildings while the lanes behind drop to one or two.

### Plots cannot get big

The 2016 notice caps development size at **200 sq m** in recommended-hanok zones, west-of-Jahamun-ro general zones and the Pirundae-ro zone; **300 sq m** east of Jahamun-ro, on Hyoja-ro and along old waterways; **400 sq m** on Jahamun-ro; and **1,200 sq m** on Sajik-ro. Designated hanok plots stay as they are. The notice gives the reason as avoiding "excessive plot sizes in order to preserve the old urban fabric".

### Chain restaurants are banned in principle

In the use tables for recommended-hanok and other zones, the 2016 notice **bans franchise-model restaurants, cafes and bakeries.** That does not mean "chains can't open here". The ban covers **franchised food and drink only**. Clothing, sportswear and cosmetics shops are not covered, which matters for the 2026 story below.

## Two waves of gentrification

![A quiet Seochon lane: a white house with barred windows and potted plants on the left, a hanok with red brick and wooden eaves on the right, and a pink flowering tree at the end of the alley](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/20.webp)

The residential lanes behind the main roads still look like this. The change is concentrated on the main roads and along the palace wall. The Hankyoreh (September 2026) describes one wave of displacement in the mid-2010s and a second in 2025-2026.

### Wave one: the 2010s and the Gungjung Jokbal case

The Hankyoreh says that in the first wave, **laundries, hair salons and corner shops gave way to small restaurants and cafes.** The case that came to symbolise it was **Gungjung Jokbal**, a braised pork-trotter restaurant. From Yonhap's report of 10 June 2018:

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 2009 | Mr Kim, who had traded in the nearby Geumcheongyo market for nearly 20 years, opens the restaurant |
| 2013 | Spends ₩35 million (about $26,000) refitting it |
| 2015 | Landlord raises monthly rent from **₩2.63m to ₩2.97m**; Kim accepts |
| **January 2016** | The building changes hands. The new owner demands **₩12m a month and a deposit of ₩100m instead of ₩30m** |
| 2016-2018 | Kim loses an eviction suit; 12 attempts at forced eviction follow, and he is badly injured in one in November 2017 |
| **7 June 2018** | Kim attacks the landlord with a hammer; arrested on 9 June on charges including attempted murder |

Korea's commercial lease law then guaranteed a tenant's right to renew for only **five years from the first contract**, and Kim was past seven. On **20 September 2018**, three months after the attack, the National Assembly extended the renewal right to **ten years** and doubled the protection period for recovering key money (the premium tenants pay each other for a going concern) from three to six months; the property news site Ddangzipgo said the change was aimed at preventing "another Seochon Gungjung Jokbal". At trial, all seven jurors found Kim not guilty of attempted murder; he was convicted of aggravated assault and sentenced to two and a half years, reduced on appeal to **two years suspended for three**, according to Lawtalk News.

### Wave two: 2025-2026 and the big brands

| When | Figure or event | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q3 2024 → Q1 2026 | Average rent for small shops **₩43,600 → ₩44,600 per sq m**; medium and large shops ₩52,100 → ₩54,200 | Energy Economy (Korea Real Estate Board) |
| Q1 and Q2 2025 | Small-shop rent change **-0.3% and 0.2%**, below Seoul and downtown averages | Hankyoreh (Korea Real Estate Board) |
| **Q2 2026** | **+1.1%** (Seoul average 0.5%, downtown 0.7%) | Hankyoreh (Korea Real Estate Board) |
| 29 March 2026 | La Cafe Gallery, run for 14 years by poet Park No-hae's charity, closes its Tongui-dong space and moves to Ogin-dong | Newspim |
| April 2026 | A main-road unit's rent demand doubles from **₩15m to ₩30m a month**, and the tenant leaves (an estate agent's account) | Newspim |
| 2026 | Cheongha Sikdang, a restaurant of nearly 50 years, closes; a running-shoe brand prepares to move in | Hankyoreh |
| July 2026 | Han Kang's bookshop Chaekbang Oneul closes | Hankyoreh, Energy Economy |

The reports name overlapping causes: **more foreign visitors, the downtown running boom (Seochon is called a "runners' mecca"), and brokerages from Gangnam and Hannam-dong canvassing landlords.** An agent told Newspim that big firms were sending letters promising to "get you hundreds of millions in key money" and "find a tenant even if you double the rent". Energy Economy found a 10 sq m ground-floor unit in Chebu-dong listed at ₩50m deposit and ₩2m a month.

### "No chains" is only half true

The 2016 notice bans franchised restaurants, cafes and bakeries, and nothing else. So sports and fashion brands could open on Seochon's main roads in 2026, and Energy Economy also reported a **Chinese milk-tea franchise** newly open on the central street. We could not establish which plan zone that shop sits in, or whether the premises pre-date the rule. What is clear is that **"Seochon has no chain stores" is no longer accurate in 2026.**

Seochon's northern edge meets the wall of Cheong Wa Dae ("the Blue House"). In four years the neighbour changed twice.

### What changed for Seochon

- **Business.** The Korea Economic Daily (1 January 2026) reported that the return of **about 1,200 staff** including the secretariat and security had lifted trade in Seochon (Changseong and Hyoja-dong) and Bukchon. One Hyoja-dong cafe introduced a 10% discount for police officers.
- **Protests and security.** Hankook Ilbo (December 2025) expected **the front of the Cheongunhyoja-dong community centre and the road beside Sarangchae** to become the main protest sites, centred on the Hyoja-dong junction. Nearby police boxes, including Ogin, moved to 24-hour operation.
- **Visiting.** Edaily (17 September 2026) reported that Cheong Wa Dae **is expected to reopen in 2027**, probably for partial tours alongside the working office, with dates undecided. As of October 2026 the grounds are closed; Sarangchae runs as an exhibition space. Chilgung booking details are in [Seochon: things to do](/en/posts/seochon-things-to-do-2026-09/).

Visitors still photograph the main building from behind Gyeongbokgung's Sinmumun gate, but Hankook Ilbo noted that stepping 20m further puts you in a security zone where photography is banned. **Don't photograph the compound walls or guards.**

## Inwangsan and Suseongdong Valley

![Red and yellow autumn maple leaves on Inwangsan fill the top of the frame, with the finial of a pavilion roof in black silhouette behind them](/images/seochon-guide-2026-09/72.webp)

Autumn colour on Inwangsan, photographed on 2 November 2016. This mountain is Seochon's western edge; its granite ridge shows at the end of almost every alley.

**Suseongdong Valley**, says the Korea Tourism Organization, was named in Joseon times for the loud, clear sound of its water and appears in Jeong Seon's *Eight Views of Jangdong*. KTO says the **Ogin pilot apartments built in 1971** buried it until they were demolished 40 years later. According to Aju Business Daily (July 2012), the city designated the site as green space in 2008, spent **₩106 billion** on restoration and reopened the valley on **11 July 2012**. It is open at all times, free, with no parking. The stone bridge, the heritage status and the Seoul City Wall trail are in [Seochon: things to do](/en/posts/seochon-things-to-do-2026-09/); leaf timing is in [Seoul autumn foliage](/en/posts/seoul-autumn-foliage-2026-09/).

## Tongin Market

According to KTO, Tongin Market began as **a public market built in June 1941 for Japanese residents of Hyoja-dong.** Stalls and shops grew around it as Seochon's population rose after the Korean War; it was registered as an officially recognised market in 2005 and named a "Seoul culture market" in 2010. There are about 70 stalls. KTO lists shops as open 07:00-21:00 and the lunchbox cafe 11:00-15:00. How the brass-coin lunchbox works, and where else to eat, is in [Seochon food](/en/posts/seochon-food-2026-09/).

## How to do it

1. **Morning** — From Gyeongbokgung Station head into Tongin-dong. The King Sejong stone is outside an optician's on the lane to Tongin Market. Market stalls open from 07:00 (KTO).
2. **Lunch** — Tongin Market's lunchbox cafe (11:00-15:00) or nearby restaurants; see [Seochon food](/en/posts/seochon-food-2026-09/).
3. **Afternoon** — Walk through Chebu, Pirun and Nuha-dong to Ogin-dong, past the Park No-soo Art Museum (closed Mondays), and up to Suseongdong Valley at the western edge.
4. **Late afternoon** — Come down along the palace-wall lane in Tongui-dong, the street changing fastest in 2026.

**Walking costs nothing.** To add Gyeongbokgung, avoid Tuesdays and check [Jongno palaces and sights](/en/posts/jongno-things-to-do-2026-09/); free palace entry in hanbok is covered in [Hanbok and free palace entry](/en/posts/hanbok-palace-2026-09/).

## Crowds and etiquette

- **These are homes.** Most hanok behind the main roads are lived in. Don't peer through gates or linger photographing doorways. As Dlive News reported, residents already live with repair bills, parking problems and fire risk.
- **Running groups.** 2025-2026 reports describe Seochon and the road in front of Cheong Wa Dae as a city running route, and Hankook Ilbo said the north pavement there has been closed since August 2025. Walk single file on narrow pavements.
- **Protests near Cheong Wa Dae.** Weekend rallies may gather around the Hyoja-dong junction. For Gwanghwamun's weekend rallies see the [Gwanghwamun guide](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/).
- **The *Culinary Class Wars* effect.** The Korea Economic Daily (January 2026) said five restaurants run by chefs from the Netflix cooking show were drawing crowds, one booked out for three months. Popular places often take reservations only.

## Where to stay

Seochon has **no registered tourist hotels**, and Seoul's licensing data shows **121 hanok guesthouses** (licensed as "hanok experience" businesses) across its 14 dong. For a large hotel you need to go down to Gwanghwamun or City Hall. The counts, the licensing rules and airport-bus stops are in [Seochon hotels](/en/posts/seochon-hotels-2026-09/); Gwanghwamun hotels are in [Gwanghwamun hotels](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-hotels-2026-09/), and a city-wide comparison in [Where to stay in Seoul](/en/posts/seoul-where-to-stay-2026-09/).

## Who it suits

**It suits you if you:**

- want to see streets drop from five floors to one as you step a single block off the main road
- like following "who lived here": Jeong Seon, Yun Dong-ju, Yi Sang, Park No-soo
- need a half-day plan for a Tuesday, when Gyeongbokgung is closed
- want to walk on to Inwangsan and Suseongdong Valley

**It may not suit you if you:**

- expect grand aristocratic courtyard houses; [Bukchon](/en/posts/bukchon-guide-2026-09/) fits better
- come on a Monday for indoor museums; most are closed
- want to tour Cheong Wa Dae; it is closed as of October 2026 (reopening reported for 2027)

## In short

- **Names**: Seochon, Utdae, Jangdong, Sejong Village (residents, from 2010), Gyeongbokgung West (official). Plan area 582,297 sq m, about a third of Bukchon.
- **History**: Centre of late-Joseon jungin literature; Jeong Seon and Kim Jeong-hui; later Yi Sang, Yun Dong-ju, Lee Jung-seob, Park No-soo. Byeoksusanjang on the Songseogwon site, demolished 1973.
- **Height**: 20m zone from 9 May 1977 → 16, 18 and 15m zones in 1978 and 1994 → **first easing June 2024 (20→24m, 16→18m)**.
- **Plan**: District plan 15 April 2010, **overhauled 14 July 2016** (designated hanok 1 floor, plots 200-1,200 sq m, franchised food banned), new notice targeted for the first half of 2027.
- **Redevelopment**: Pirun 1, Chebu 1, Nuha 1 lifted in 2015, Ogin 1 in 2017; Sajik 2's cancellation voided in court.
- **Gentrification**: The 2018 Gungjung Jokbal case → ten-year lease renewal right. Q2 2026 rents +1.1%, Han Kang's bookshop and a 50-year-old restaurant gone, sportswear brands on the main road.
- **Cheong Wa Dae**: Opened 10 May 2022 → tours suspended 1 August 2025 → **president back 29 December 2025** → partial reopening targeted for 2027 (report).

In one line: **Seochon stayed low because it was not allowed to grow, and when that low skyline became valuable, the roofs stayed put while the shops and people beneath them changed twice.**
