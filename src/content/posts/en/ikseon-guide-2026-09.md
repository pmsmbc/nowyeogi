On 15 September 2026, the London-based guide Time Out published its list of the "coolest neighbourhoods in the world". Number one was **Jongno 3-ga in Seoul**, and according to Korean coverage the places Time Out described in most detail were **Ikseon-dong's century-old hanok alleys and Seosulla-gil, the lane along the Jongmyo Shrine wall** (Segye Ilbo, Yonhap). Three days later, Le Monde diplomatique's Korean edition reports, the journalist who had first recommended the area to Time Out in 2021 wrote in the Guardian that he was "devastated". The same few blocks drew a trophy and a worry in the same week.

The short version: Ikseon-dong is **a 1920s-30s estate of small, mass-built hanok (traditional Korean houses) that was slated for demolition, was formally "kept" by a city decision in 2018, and then became one of the fastest-changing commercial streets in Seoul**. This guide rebuilds that story from the original planning notices and dated reporting. For individual sights see [things to do in Ikseon-dong](/en/posts/ikseon-things-to-do-2026-09/); for food, [where to eat in Ikseon-dong](/en/posts/ikseon-food-2026-09/); for beds, [where to stay in Ikseon-dong](/en/posts/ikseon-hotels-2026-09/).

This is inside the preservation zone around 166 Ikseon-dong: hanok roofs, paving blocks underfoot and a tangle of cables overhead. That mix is today's Ikseon-dong.

Newspaper figures of "800-plus shops in Ikseon-dong" use a much wider boundary around Jongno 3-ga, so the two numbers describe different places.

## Names and ground: a royal house, a famous gisaeng house, a Goryeo lane

### A name from 1914

According to the Seoul Hanok Portal, the name **Ikseon-dong dates from 1914**, when the colonial administration merged old neighbourhoods and combined "ik" from the village of Ikdong with "seon" from the Joseon ward of Jeongseon-bang. A City of Seoul article traces Ikdong itself to the long wing-like servants' quarters (ikrang) of **Nudonggung**, a royal residence; the character for "wing" was later swapped for a homophone meaning "to increase".

The Hanok Portal says Nudonggung was **the private house and shrine of the father of King Cheoljong**, the 25th Joseon king, and that descendants of Cheoljong's elder brother lived there into the colonial period. The land around it later became the hanok estate.

### A lane older than Joseon

Korean Wikipedia cites Hanyang University emeritus professor Choi Jong-hyun: the main Joseon roads here (Jong-ro, Donhwamun-ro and the Pimatgil back lanes) were laid out straight, so **the alleys that cut across them diagonally are likely older, from the Goryeo period**. One of them is **Donhwamun-ro 11na-gil**, which runs north-south through Ikseon-dong; the Hanok Portal also calls it a lane "continuing from the Goryeo era" and identifies Donhwamun-ro 11ga-gil one row in as a trace of Pimatgil. That is why the hanok alleys here are not a grid.

### The Ojinam site

The Hanok Portal says **34-8 Ikseon-dong**, now a tourist hotel, was once **Ojinam**, one of the three best-known yojeong (high-end hostess restaurants) of the 1970s-80s, Seoul's registered restaurant number one, and **a place where the 4 July 1972 North-South Joint Statement was discussed**. It was demolished when the hotel went up in 2010; its timbers were rebuilt in Buam-dong as the Mugyewon cultural centre. "Ojinam site" is a stop on Jongno-gu's official alley walk.

## Jeong Se-gwon and Geonyangsa: who built these houses

![Ikseon-dong seen from above: a dense sea of black tiled and canvas hanok roofs, ringed by hotels and office blocks under a blue sky](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/07.webp)

From above, Ikseon-dong is an **island of hanok roofs** among office blocks. The man behind the island was **Jeong Se-gwon (1888-1965)**.

### What the City of Seoul says

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| 10 April 1888 | Born in Goseong, South Gyeongsang |
| **1919** | Moves to Seoul and settles in Gye-dong |
| **Around 1920** | Founds the property development company **Geonyangsa** |
| **Late 1920s** | Buys **the largely neglected land around Nudonggung** in Ikseon-dong |
| **1935** | **Dozens of hanok** go up here |
| 1990 | Posthumously awarded the Order of Merit for National Foundation |

The city describes him as someone "regarded as Korea's first modern developer", who worried that Japanese residents were spreading north from Namchon (today's Myeong-dong and Chungmu-ro) into Jongno and Bukchon and set out to **build hanok for Koreans**. Profits went to support the nationalist Singanhoe and the Korean Language Society. He bought land in Bukchon, Ikseon-dong, Hyehwa-dong, Changsin-dong and elsewhere, subdivided it and supplied what were then called "Joseon houses" in bulk; his standard plan put rooms around a central wooden-floored hall and **brought the toilet indoors**.

The Hanok Portal is more specific: Jeong bought **lots 166, 33 and 19 in Ikseon-dong, among others, split them into small plots and built small and medium hanok to sell or let on jeonse** (Korea's lump-sum deposit lease). In other words, Ikseon-dong was **never an aristocrats' quarter; it was a speculative estate of modest family homes**.

### The numbers do not agree

Sources disagree on how many houses there are and when they were built, so they are listed side by side here.

| Source | Built | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City of Seoul (Jeong Se-gwon article) | Land bought late 1920s, **1935** | "Dozens" |
| Seoul Hanok Portal | **Before the 1930s** | **118** |
| Dong-A Ilbo, January 2018 (citing the city) | **1920s-1950s** | 119 |
| Newspim, December 2018 | — | 110 in three alleys |
| Chosun Biz, April 2017 | "Early 1920s" | About 110 households |

**There is no official figure for how many of today's houses Jeong himself built.** The 118 is the number of hanok that survive, not the number he put up.

### A wall that disappeared

The Scoop, a Korean weekly, reports that a wall describing Jeong's work once stood where Samil-daero 30-gil turns into the hanok alleys, and that it was torn down during redevelopment. Today there is little on the ground to tell visitors who built the place. The city announced a Jeong Se-gwon commemoration project in January 2018 (Metro Seoul); what, if anything, it left in Ikseon-dong itself could not be confirmed.

## From yojeong quarter to run-down commercial zone

The Hanok Portal says the area became a roadside commercial district under Japanese rule and that **in 1970 the city zoned the whole downtown as commercial**, turning Ikseon-dong into an "entertainment centre" tied to the yojeong trade. Korean Wikipedia adds that many performers, including female pansori singers, lived here in the colonial era, that the yojeong business later faded, and that from the late twentieth century the area was **also a home for Seoul's LGBTQ community**.

That zoning is still in force. The 2018 plan lists **all 31,121.5 m² as general commercial land**, with a floor-area-ratio ceiling of 600% under city ordinance. On paper you could build tall here; the height rules below are what stop it.

### Jongsam and Donui-dong

Just south, in Donui-dong, another history overlaps. Korean Wikipedia says a red-light district known as **"Jongsam"** grew up after liberation between Tapgol Park and Jongno 5-ga and was cleared in **autumn 1968**; **from the 1970s, jjokbang** (tiny rented rooms) took its place. The buildings are typically **hanok divided into small rooms with a second brick storey added on top**. Today's situation is covered in the Donui-dong section below.

The four items of 10 May 2018:

1. **Notice 2018-132**: the redevelopment zone is **lifted**.
2. **Notice 2018-142**: the **Ikseon district unit plan** (31,121.5 m²) is adopted.

That regeneration plan had four strategies, two of them here: **"palace culture come down to the market: Nagwon to Ikseon"** and **"Seosulla-gil, heart of the precious-metals industry"**. Today's Ikseon-dong and Seosulla-gil were named in a plan before they became what they are.

### Did residents "save" Ikseon-dong?

The official record shows three things: **the planning committee pushed for hanok preservation in 2010, the redevelopment committee dissolved in 2014, and the city refused to lift the zone until preservation rules were in place**. OhmyNews (2017) actually shows **landowners hanging banners against the height limits** at a residents' meeting. The popular story that residents united to stop redevelopment does not match these records.

A Seoul official quoted in the same article said that if the plan had been drawn up quickly after 2014, "the current rapid change could have been prevented". In the **four-year gap between the 2014 deferral and the 2018 notice**, the shops arrived first.

## What the district plan actually says

Ikseon-dong's low rooflines and tiny plots are not just charm; they are **written into the 2018 notice**. Here are the parts visitors will notice.

### Height by zone

| Zone | Height limit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hanok preservation zone** (around lot 166) | **Hanok: 1 storey. Non-hanok: 2 storeys (8m)** | Up to 3 storeys (8m) for non-hanok with district approval |
| General management zone | Hanok 2 storeys; others 3 storeys (12m) | 4 storeys (12m) with approval |
| Street character zones | 3 storeys (12m) | Split by road width under / over 6m |
| **Donhwamun-ro and Taehwagwan-gil zones** | **5 storeys (20m)** | Main-road frontage |

So on land zoned for 600% floor area, the preservation zone is effectively limited to **one or two storeys**. Buildings rise as you move out to the main roads.

### Plots cannot be merged

- **No merging of plots anywhere in the area.**
- Along Donhwamun-ro and Taehwagwan-gil, **maximum development size is 330 m²** (with exceptions for plots already larger and for new hanok).
- In the preservation, general management and street character zones, development happens **plot by plot**; joint development of several plots is not allowed except where designated or for hanok within 330 m².

The small plots Jeong Se-gwon cut in the 1920s-30s are, in effect, **frozen by law**.

### Rewards for building a hanok

- **Building coverage**: 60% by default, **relaxed by 20 percentage points (to 80%)** if hanok form and appearance rules are followed.
- **Parking**: **100% relaxation of on-site parking requirements** for hanok. Plots facing Donhwamun-ro, Donhwamun-ro 11-gil and Pimatgil may not have vehicle access at all.
- **Use**: a detached hanok in the preservation or general zones can host uses allowed in neighbourhood commercial zones.
- **Repairs**: Newsis (March 2018) reported repair grants of **up to ₩180 million (about $135,000)** per hanok.

On 24 December 2020, Ikseon and eight other hanok areas in Jongno and Seongbuk became **"architectural asset promotion districts"** under national law (Financial News), and in May 2026 the city said it would **scrap the ecological-area-ratio requirement** for new hanok in these districts (Seoul Shinmun), which reported that the building-coverage allowance for hanok there reaches **90%**.

### Coffee is not a "recommended use"

An earlier version of this guide got this backwards, so here it is in full. In the plan's table of **recommended uses**, the entry for "rest restaurants and bakeries" lists the recommended sub-uses as **traditional tea houses, hanjeongsik (set-meal) restaurants and traditional pubs**. The same notice then defines a traditional tea house:

> A business that cooks and sells only porridges such as jatjuk and kkaejuk and teas such as ginseng tea and ssanghwa tea (**excluding coffee**)

So **Ikseon-dong's many cafés are not there because the city recommended them**. Cafés are allowed in a commercial zone anyway; what the plan recommended was non-coffee traditional tea. A "traditional pub" is likewise defined as selling Korean specialty liquor, "excluding beer and Western spirits". Other recommended uses include craft shops, antiques, hanbok (traditional dress) and Korean instrument shops, traditional workshops, performance venues and museums, and **hanok stays and foreigner-oriented urban homestays**.

### Chains are banned in principle

In the preservation, general and street character zones, the plan bans **franchise or chain restaurants, cafés and bakeries** as "gentrification-inducing uses", unless a residents' agreement allows them. Yonhap (21 March 2025) reports that Jongno-gu has restricted new franchise businesses in five district plan areas including Bukchon and Ikseon-dong since 2016, and has now allowed existing **convenience stores and coffee franchises only** to change brand within the same use and floor area, after owners complained of tougher contract terms.

Signs are regulated too: **avoid primary colours, and no flashing neon or electronic displays** unless approved by the district's outdoor advertising committee.

### Later changes

The most recent amendment is **Jongno-gu notice 2025-172 of 13 November 2025**, which changed the list of **prohibited building uses** set in 2018. The attached text could not be downloaded, so what exactly changed is not confirmed here.

Three footnotes:

- **The houses changed shape.** The Scoop notes many hanok that kept wooden posts but lost their stone plinths or tiles, or swapped walls for glass, which does not meet the repair-grant rules.

### Ikseon-dong in 2026

Today Ikseon-dong is **a daytime hanok café street joined to a night-time outdoor drinking street**. In October 2025 Jongno-gu merged **Galmaegi-golmok in Ikseon-dong and Donhwamun-ro 11-gil into one "coexistence street"** with an agreement covering about 70 businesses (Newsis), and in September 2026 it tightened the rules for 77 street and shop operators trading 18:00-04:00 (Yonhap). The details are in [Jongno 3-ga pocha street](/en/posts/jongno-pocha-2026-09/).

Time Out's ranking leans on that night scene. Newsis, citing CNN, quotes Time Out travel editor Grace Beard saying Jongno 3-ga rose **from third place in 2021 to first in 2026** thanks to "late-night outdoor dining and independent shops". Second was a district in Rabat, Morocco; third Athens; fourth Los Angeles' Chinatown; fifth Glasgow. Le Monde diplomatique noted that Time Out's 2021 entry highlighted the area's **LGBTQ community**, and that the Guardian writer described it as a refuge for older people and jjokbang residents, concluding that "the weight of praise is shifting from people to scenery".

## Seosulla-gil: the lane along the Jongmyo wall

![Blue umbrellas hang above an Ikseon-dong alley, with potted plants and menu boards in front of hanok shops on both sides and the paved lane running straight into the distance](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/12.webp)

Cross Donhwamun-ro east from Ikseon-dong and you reach the west wall of Jongmyo Shrine. The lane along it is **Seosulla-gil**.

### Name and extent

The Korea Tourism Organization describes Seosulla-gil as the road **from 150-3 Jong-ro (Jongno 3-ga) to 26 Gwonnong-dong**. Joseon night patrols (sullagun) walked it, and the name means "the road west of the patrol office" that guarded Jongmyo. Segye Ilbo and Dong-A Ilbo, citing the city, give its length as **820m**.

Dong-A Ilbo (February 2026) says the city **limited nearby buildings to two storeys** and required wood and tile on facades, and that by city figures **average monthly sales for nearby small businesses rose about 2.5 times, from ₩1.72 million in 2020 to ₩4.34 million in Q1 2025**.

### A jewellery quarter

The south end of Seosulla-gil runs into Jongno 3-ga's jewellery wholesale arcades. Jongno-gu's official walking route calls it **"Seosulla-gil, dressed up with jewellery"**, and Time Out mentions **workshops, jewellery studios and wine bars** along the wall (as quoted by Segye Ilbo). Next door in Myo-dong, **Dansungsa**, which Korean Wikipedia describes as Korea's first proper permanent cinema (1907), was renamed Dansung Gold Building in 2016 with a jewellery centre on the ground floor.

Jongmyo's own rules (closed Tuesdays; timed guided entry on Mondays, Thursdays and Fridays) and the high-rise dispute opposite the shrine are covered in the [Jongno guide](/en/posts/jongno-guide-2026-09/).

South of Ikseon-dong, beside Jongno 3-ga Station, Donui-dong has **Seoul's second-largest jjokbang village** (an area of tiny one-room rentals for very low-income residents). Only the facts needed to understand the area are given here; it is not presented as a place to visit.

| 2003 survey | About 90 buildings, 651 rooms | Korean Wikipedia |

## Photos and noise: Jongno-gu's four promises

![A narrow Ikseon-dong hanok alley. A group of visitors walks between green tin eaves and brick walls, with pale blue and grey buildings and utility poles behind](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/16.webp)

Jongno-gu's tourism site posts this on the walking-route page that includes Ikseon-dong (translated):

> Parts of the alley walking routes are homes where residents actually live.
>
> Promise 1. Don't startle or bother residents with shouting or indiscriminate photography.
> Promise 2. If you create rubbish, take it with you.
> Promise 3. Mind even small noises, and never use loudspeakers or microphones.
> Promise 4. Don't peer into or secretly photograph residents' hanok or the inside of their homes.

The alleys are so narrow that **shop doors and private front gates sit side by side**. OhmyNews reported back in 2017 that remaining residents were "exposed without protection to cameras thrust at them by tourists". In practice, promise 4 means: **if a hanok has no shop sign, don't look in, even if the door is open**.

**The rules differ from Bukchon.** Visiting-hour limits and fines apply to the Bukchon "red zone" (see the [Bukchon guide](/en/posts/bukchon-guide-2026-09/)); no notice of a similar time limit for Ikseon-dong could be found as of this update. The four promises above are guidance.

## What we got wrong

The first version of this guide (22 September 2026) contained errors and weakly sourced claims. After rereading the original notices:

**One: we wrote that "cafés and bakeries are uses recommended by the city".** The recommended sub-uses are **traditional tea houses, set-meal restaurants and traditional pubs**, and a traditional tea house is defined as selling tea **excluding coffee**. Meanwhile **chain cafés and bakeries are banned**. We had it backwards.

**Two: we presented "no plot merging, maximum development size 330 m²" as a rule for the whole area.** The merging ban is area-wide, but **the 330 m² cap applies to the Donhwamun-ro and Taehwagwan-gil zones**; the preservation zone and others develop **plot by plot**.

**Three: we called the non-hanok limit of two storeys (8m) "effectively single-storey".** Two storeys are two storeys. The one-storey limit applies only to **hanok in the preservation zone**.

**Four: we gave the redevelopment zone's designation date as 29 April 2004.** The lifting notice (2018-132) cites designation notice **2004-124 of 6 May 2004**. We could not find a source for 29 April.

**Five: we said flatly that the houses were "built in the 1930s for Koreans".** City sources variously say "land bought in the late 1920s, dozens of houses in 1935", "118 hanok from before the 1930s" and "119 hanok from the 1920s-1950s". The table above sets them out.

**Six: we left out the 2010 planning committee decision.** The committee had already asked for **a hanok-preservation plan** before the redevelopment committee dissolved in 2014.

**Seven: we called the 13 November 2025 change a "correction" of prohibited uses.** The notice is titled a "change"; its content is unconfirmed.

**Eight: "you can walk all the alleys in 30 minutes" had no source and has been removed.** Adding Seosulla-gil changes the distance anyway.

## How to do it

The area **changes character by time of day**. Using only confirmed times:

1. **Morning**: the rooflines and alley layout are easiest to see before shops open. It is also when residents go to work and shop, so don't block the lanes.
2. **Lunch to afternoon**: the hanok café and restaurant streets; see [where to eat in Ikseon-dong](/en/posts/ikseon-food-2026-09/). Then cross Donhwamun-ro to **Seosulla-gil**, car-free from 10:00 at weekends.
3. **Late afternoon**: walk north along the wall to Yulgok-ro and the Jongmyo-Changgyeonggung woodland path reopened in 2022.
4. **After 18:00**: the yajang street sets up in Galmaegi-golmok and Donhwamun-ro 11-gil (18:00-04:00 per Jongno-gu). Weekend car-free hours on Seosulla-gil run to 22:00.

**Walking is free.** Jongmyo costs ₩1,000 for adults and closes on Tuesdays (Royal Palaces and Tombs Center; see [things to do in Jongno](/en/posts/jongno-things-to-do-2026-09/)).

## Crowds and safety

- **The lanes are narrow.** The preservation zone is only 7,565.9 m², so on weekend afternoons some stretches become single file. With a pushchair or large suitcase, use Donhwamun-ro or Samil-daero instead.
- **At night the yajang street has toilet and smoking problems**; Jongno-gu itself named more toilets and designated smoking areas as priorities. Find a toilet before you settle in.
- **Don't photograph the jjokbang lanes or private homes.** The four promises above are the official guidance.
- **Autumn 2026 may be busier than usual** after the Time Out ranking. No visitor figures since the announcement have been published yet.

## Where to stay

Ikseon-dong and Nagwon-dong have several large hotels with "Insadong" in their names, while the preservation zone mixes in hanok stays and urban homestays; the district plan bans ordinary lodging in the preservation and general zones and recommends only hanok stays and foreigner homestays. ## Who it suits

**It suits you if:**

- You like low hanok rooflines and maze-like lanes
- You want to see how a single planning notice shapes a neighbourhood
- You are visiting Jongmyo or Changdeokgung and want to add half a day, ending at Seosulla-gil or the yajang street

**It may not suit you if:**

- You want spacious aristocratic houses with courtyards: [Bukchon](/en/posts/bukchon-guide-2026-09/) fits better. Ikseon-dong was a modest housing estate
- You want a quiet hanok walk: avoid weekend afternoons and evenings
- You plan to drive: there is almost nowhere to park

## In short

- **Ikseon-dong**: a 1914 name on the site of a royal residence. "The hanok village" is the **7,565.9 m² preservation zone** around lot 166.
- **Jeong Se-gwon and Geonyangsa**: land bought in the late 1920s, dozens of hanok by 1935 (City of Seoul); 118 hanok survive (Hanok Portal).
- **Twenty years of redevelopment**: designated 2004 → preservation urged 2010 → committee dissolved and lifting deferred 2014 → **zone lifted, district plan and hanok areas all published on 10 May 2018**.
- **The rules**: one storey for hanok and two (8m) for others in the preservation zone, no plot merging, chain restaurants, cafés and bakeries banned, **recommended tea houses exclude coffee**.
- **Shops**: one in 2014, about 50 in 2017, about 330 in 2018; 49.4% of spending from people in their 20s and 30s in Q1 2026.
- **Seosulla-gil**: 820m along Jongmyo's west wall, rebuilt from 2010, **car-free 10:00-22:00 at weekends**, small-business sales up 2.5 times.
- **Donui-dong jjokbang village**: 81 buildings, about 500 residents. Neighbours, not a sight.
- **2026**: Time Out's world number one (Jongno 3-ga), with Ikseon-dong and Seosulla-gil at its centre.

In one line: **Ikseon-dong was not simply left standing; in 2018 the city decided to keep it, and that decision protected the roofs and the plots while leaving who lives there and what is sold to the market.**
