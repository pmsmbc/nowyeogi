Ikseon-dong is **the neighbourhood in Seoul where redevelopment fell through**.

Euljiro, right next door, is a place where the city has announced it will "demolish the old and dangerous arcade blocks", and demolition is actually under way. Ikseon-dong is the exact opposite. **In 2018 its redevelopment designation was lifted, and on the same day three preservation measures were attached.**

This post sets out that process, and what the place is like now, from official records.

![A narrow Ikseon-dong alley, power lines tangled above the tiled hanok roofs on either side and an officetel block rising behind](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/02.webp)

## Who built it

The 1930s, under Japanese colonial rule.

This is how the city's official material describes it.

> Jeong Se-gwon was **deeply concerned** that the Japanese, then settling into Seoul in earnest, would expand their residential areas in Namchon and Chungmuro and **push on into Jongno and Bukchon**. As a countermeasure, he threw himself into **building hanok for Korean residents**.

> In **the late 1920s** Jeong Se-gwon bought up **the land around Nudonggung palace in Ikseon-dong**, which had been all but abandoned, **and in 1935 dozens of hanok went up there**.

**Jeong Se-gwon** founded a property development firm, **the Geonyangsa company**, around 1920. The city describes him as **"the first modern developer"** in Korea.

He spent what he earned from hanok development supporting the Singanhoe and the Korean Language Society, and **in 1990 he was posthumously awarded the Order of Merit for National Foundation**.

### A note of caution here

This is a romantic story and an easy one to inflate. Here is what is official and what is not.

- **Official city account** - the founding of the Geonyangsa company, the intent to block Japanese expansion into Bukchon and Jongno, the purchase of Ikseon-dong and the 1935 development, the support for the independence movement, the state decoration
- **A researcher's estimate** - the figure of "around 170 houses in the single year 1929" is cited from a book by Professor Kim Kyung-min. It is not a city survey figure
- **Not confirmed** - **there is no official figure for exactly how many hanok in Ikseon-dong Jeong Se-gwon built**

The number of hanok left in Ikseon-dong today is **118, by the Seoul Hanok Portal's count**. That is "how many remain", not "how many he built".

### What an urban hanok is

It is not a traditional hanok. It is **collective housing: several units built tight together on a small plot**. The courtyards are small and the alleys are narrow.

So Ikseon-dong was **a mass-produced housing estate for ordinary people** from the start, not a district of aristocratic houses.

## Why the redevelopment fell through

This is the heart of the post, and a timeline makes it clear.

| Date | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 29 April 2004 | **The Ikseon urban redevelopment zone is designated** |
| 3 January 2014 | **Approval of the redevelopment association's preparatory committee is cancelled** |
| **1 October 2014** | **The city planning committee defers the proposal to lift the zone** |
| 18 April 2018 | The city planning committee approves the proposal as drafted |
| **10 May 2018** | **Lifting, district unit plan and hanok concentration area designation, all gazetted at once** |

**The decisive moment is not 2018 but 1 October 2014.** The city planning committee did not simply release the zone. It demanded this.

> **Before** the redevelopment zone is lifted, management measures such as designation of a hanok concentration area and of a district unit plan area are to be prepared and the proposal resubmitted

It means **"you may lift it, but lay the preservation measures first"**. Only when those measures were complete, on 10 May 2018, was the zone lifted, and **three things were gazetted the same day**.

- The urban redevelopment zone was **lifted**
- **The Ikseon district unit plan** was decided
- **The Ikseon hanok concentration area (31,121.5 square metres) and hanok preservation zone (7,565.9 square metres)** were designated

### An honest note

You will often read that "the residents fought off the redevelopment", but **no official document supporting that could be found**.

The official record says two things. **Approval of the redevelopment association's preparatory committee was cancelled in 2014**, and **the city planning committee attached preservation conditions**. The project ran aground first, and the administration attached conditions to that.

## The alleys are narrow because of regulation

![An information board at the entrance to an Ikseon-dong alley, with hanok eaves and shopfronts running on beyond it](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/05.webp)

Once you know that the narrowness of these alleys is not atmosphere but **a result fixed in law**, the neighbourhood looks different.

Here is the district unit plan notice itself.

> **Merging plots not permitted - applies to: the whole zone**

> Maximum development scale **330 square metres or less**

**Land cannot be combined and the development unit is capped at 330 square metres.** Putting up a large building is structurally impossible.

Heights are set too. **In the hanok preservation zone, hanok may be one storey or lower and non-hanok two storeys (8m) or lower.** In practice, single storey.

### Building a hanok comes with rewards

The design is clever. **Rather than forbidding demolition, it makes demolition a losing move.**

Build as a hanok and you get **a 20% relaxation of the building coverage ratio, a 100% exemption from the parking requirement, and a relaxation of permitted uses**. Tear a hanok down and put up an ordinary building and all of that disappears, leaving only the limits on height and scale.

### The cafes are not a loophole

This is widely misunderstood too. The district unit plan's list of **"recommended uses"** reads as follows.

> Refreshment stands and bakeries → **traditional teahouses, Korean set-menu restaurants, traditional bars**
> Detached houses → **the hanok-stay business, urban homestay**

**Cafes and bakeries are uses the city recommended.** Ikseon-dong turning into cafes was not an exploited gap in the rules but the planned direction.

The most recent change is **a Jongno-gu notice of 13 November 2025**, correcting the list of prohibited building uses.

## A day here

Jongno-gu has an official alley walking course: **"Ikseon-dong / the road that links the generations / 2 hours"**.

> Ikseon-dong Hanok Village · Urisori Library · the Jewellery Support Centre · Donhwamun Gugakdang traditional music hall · the senior-friendly street · Seosulla-gil

The point of the walk, as the district office puts it, is an interesting one.

> a road of exchange **linking the generations, between the young people of Ikseon-dong and the older people** of the Jongno 3-ga area

Ikseon-dong is small. **Thirty minutes is enough to walk every alley.** So it makes sense to pair it with the wider Jongno 3-ga area and give the two half a day. Changdeokgung Palace and Jongmyo Shrine are within walking distance.

## People live here

![An Ikseon-dong alley of low continuous hanok rooflines, with a wooden deck laid in front of a mint-green shop](/images/ikseon-guide-2026-09/06.webp)

Jongno-gu puts this notice on its Ikseon-dong page.

> **Parts of the alley walking course are places where residents actually live.**
>
> **Promise 1.** Do not startle or disturb residents by shouting or taking photographs carelessly.
> **Promise 2.** If you make rubbish, you must take it with you.
> **Promise 3.** Mind even small noises, and never use a loudspeaker or a microphone.
> **Promise 4.** Do not peer into residents' hanok or homes, or photograph them without permission.

The alleys are so narrow that **you cannot tell a cafe door from somebody's front gate**. That is what the fourth promise is for.

**Do not confuse this with Bukchon.** The visiting-hour restrictions and the fines belong to the Bukchon red zone; **there is no such regulation in Ikseon-dong**. There is guidance, though.

## Who this suits

**It suits you if:**

- You like narrow alleys and low rooflines
- You are interested in how a city survives and how it disappears
- You want to add half a day to a Jongno itinerary

**It does not suit you if:**

- You are expecting large, grand hanok houses. This was a housing estate for ordinary people
- You want quiet. Avoid the evening
- You are coming for Ikseon-dong alone. That is thirty minutes

## Wrapping up

- It is an estate of **urban hanok built in the 1930s for Korean residents**.
- **Cancellation of the association's preparatory committee in 2014** left the project aground, and **the city planning committee attached preservation conditions**.
- **On 10 May 2018 the lifting, the district unit plan and the hanok concentration area** were gazetted on the same day.
- **Merging plots is not permitted, and the maximum development scale is 330 square metres.** The narrow alleys are the result of regulation.
- **Build as a hanok and you get building-coverage relief and a parking exemption.**
- **The cafes are a recommended use under the district unit plan.**
- **141m from exit 4 of Jongno 3-ga Station**, and people live here.

In one line: **Ikseon-dong was not left behind. It was decided upon.**
