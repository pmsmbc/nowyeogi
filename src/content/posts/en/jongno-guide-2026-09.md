On 29 September 2026, Jongno District announced that the British magazine Time Out had placed **the Jongno 3-ga area first** in its list of "the world's 40 coolest neighbourhoods" for 2026 (Time Out itself published the list on 15 September). The same press release came with plans for designated smoking areas, more public toilets and a crackdown on illegal street-trading expansion. Two days later, on 1 October, when Statistics Korea published its annual report on older people, a wire photo showed **a queue of elderly men waiting for a free lunch outside Tapgol Park**. The two scenes are a few minutes' walk apart.

The short version: Jongno is **a Joseon-era main street and market that has been painted over, layer by layer, for more than 600 years**. A royal ancestral shrine (Jongmyo), two palaces (Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung), the park where the 1919 independence declaration was read, a traditional crafts street, a square for the city's older men, a night street of plastic stools for young visitors, and a theatre district all sit in one district. This guide puts that Jongno back together from official records and dated reporting.

## Joseon's main street: the bell, Unjongga and Pimatgol

### Where people gathered like clouds

According to Korean Wikipedia, Jong-ro was **a planned road laid out when the capital moved to Hanyang, running from the ministries' avenue (today's Sejong-daero) to Heunginjimun gate**. It was 56 cheok wide by the Joseon builder's measure, with a 2-cheok channel on each side. Because people crowded it "like clouds", it was called **Unjongga** (雲從街), and the licensed royal monopoly shops, the **Yuguijeon**, lined it in rowhouse form to the tune of **881 bays**.

The Seoul Shinmun (21 August 2026) adds that the Yuguijeon dealt in silk, paper, fish, ramie, satin and cotton, and that if Sejong-daero was the street of government offices, Jong-ro, laid at right angles to it, was where goods from all eight provinces arrived. The bell that named the street hung where Bosingak stands today; that story, and New Year's Eve bell-ringing, are told in the [Gwanghwamun guide](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/).

### Pimatgol, the alley for avoiding horses

Directly behind the main street ran **Pimatgol**, literally "the lane for avoiding horses": a narrow back street where commoners could walk without having to bow to high officials riding by. The Seoul Shinmun notes that after liberation it became a haunt of writers, journalists and artists, and under military rule a place where protesters hid from police.

| When | What happened | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Late Korean Empire to colonial era | Tapgol Park and the Hwashin department store cut parts of the lane | Wikipedia (Korean) |
| **Around 1974** | Widening Jong-ro for subway Line 1 erased part of it | Seoul Shinmun, Wikipedia |
| After that | A back lane survived from behind Kyobo Book Centre (1-ga) to Jongno 3-ga, lined with hangover-soup, grilled-fish and mung-bean pancake joints | Seoul Shinmun |
| **2009** | **Cheongjin-dong redevelopment** demolished much of it; after criticism, Seoul decided to restore what had gone and preserve what remained | Seoul Shinmun, Wikipedia |

Go to Cheongjin-dong north of Jongno 1-ga today and you will find office towers where the alley was. Maeil Business reports that some old restaurants survived by moving into the new buildings, such as Gwanghwamun D Tower, Le Meilleur Jongno Tower and Gran Seoul. So a "Pimatgol" restaurant you find online may now be a ground-floor unit in a tower. Surviving old-timers are in [what to eat in Jongno](/en/posts/jongno-food-2026-09/).

## Tapgol Park: where 1919 began, and where Seoul's old men gather

![Samilmun, the main gate of Tapgol Park, seen from inside. A statue with a raised arm stands on the left, and visitors pass in front of the tiled-roof gate with red pillars, office towers behind](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/13.webp)

### From temple site to park

According to the Korea Tourism Organization (KTO), Tapgol Park sits on the site of **Wongaksa**, a temple built under King Sejo. In 1897 a British adviser to the Korean government, McLeavy Brown, laid it out as a park, and it **opened as a public park in 1920**, making it Seoul's first modern park. Its name changed from "Pagoda Park" to Tapgol Park on 28 May 1992. Inside are the **National Treasure ten-storey pagoda of Wongaksa**, the Wongaksa stele, an octagonal pavilion and a memorial with reliefs of the independence movement. Yonhap (August 2025) notes that the park was designated a **historic site in 1991**, so everything inside and outside its walls is a protected heritage zone.

### 1 March 1919

What happened here on the first day of the March First Movement happened in two places. Following Korean Wikipedia, the 33 national representatives, wanting to avoid bloodshed, changed their meeting place and declared independence at **Taehwagwan, a restaurant in Insadong**. Meanwhile at **2pm, at the original meeting point, Tapgol Park**, students gathered, read the declaration aloud and marched into the streets shouting "Manse!" The two spots are about 300m apart. Walk Insadong and Tapgol Park on the same day and you cover that distance.

### 2024-2026: a park being changed

For decades Tapgol Park has been a gathering place for older men. Jongno District has been blunt about the side effects: in Yonhap's report (25 August 2025), the district described "frequent drinking, loud singing, public urination and littering, and even drunken quarrels and violence".

| When | What | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 March 2024 | "Tapgol Park improvement" declared at the independence day ceremony | Yonhap, August 2025 |
| **23 August 2024** | The long-closed **north and east gates reopened** | Yonhap, August 2024 |
| **31 July 2025** | Joint enforcement with police against **baduk and janggi (board games), smoking, drinking and singing, and trading** | Yonhap, August 2025 |
| August 2025 | Board-game tables and chairs removed; players pointed to a nearby seniors' welfare centre | Yonhap, August 2025 |

Two things matter for visitors. **No smoking inside the park, and drinking is also subject to enforcement.** And many older men still gather around the north gate and nearby alleys; avoid photographing faces head-on. Opening hours per KTO are **09:00-18:00 from March to October and 09:00-17:00 from November to February**, free.

## Nagwon Arcade and Jongno 3-ga: two generations on one block

### An arcade built over a road

The building straddling Samil-daero beyond Tapgol Park's east wall is **Nagwon Arcade** (Nagwon Sangga). Korean Wikipedia says a plan to clear the unlicensed housing and Nagwon Market came out in 1967; construction **began on 26 October 1967, the building received its use permit on 15 September 1968, and it was finished on 12 December 1970**. Traffic runs underneath it. Several reports, including the Financial News (2017), say "completed in 1969", so we list all three dates.

It became a musical-instrument arcade later. Wikipedia says that when the arcade along Tapgol Park's wall was **demolished in July 1983**, its music shops moved into Nagwon Arcade; from the 1990s, karaoke and backing machines cut demand for live musicians and the arcade declined. It was still recognised for its value as an instrument market and named a **Seoul Future Heritage in 2013**.

### Jongno 3-ga, world number one in 2026

The lanes northeast of Nagwon Arcade are the **Jongno 3-ga night street** now packed with foreign visitors. Jongno District's statement of 29 September 2026 (Yonhap) says:

- The Jongno 3-ga area ranked **first in Time Out's "40 coolest neighbourhoods in the world" for 2026**
- The district designated Donhwamun-ro 11-gil (November 2024) and Ikseon-dong's Galmaegi alley (October 2025) as **"coexistence streets"**, bringing the tent bars into a legal framework instead of removing them
- **77 businesses, 24 street stalls and 53 shops**, now trade under this system
- Street stalls are limited to 5.5m x 3m, shops to one row in front, and **trading hours to 18:00-04:00**
- Next: constant enforcement against illegal expansion, designated smoking areas, more privately run public toilets

On the same day the Kyunghyang Shinmun described the street as "stained by smoking and public urination". Popularity has come faster than toilets. Stalls and the difference between this alley and Songhae-gil are in [Jongno 3-ga pocha street](/en/posts/jongno-pocha-2026-09/); the hanok lanes next door are in the [Ikseon-dong guide](/en/posts/ikseon-guide-2026-09/).

On one block, **the afternoon belongs to the old men of Tapgol Park and the night to young visitors at the tent bars**. That timetable is the key to understanding Jongno 3-ga.

## Insadong: twenty years as Korea's first cultural district

![Insadong-gil lined with ginkgo trees. Green leaves form a tunnel over a gently curving stone street, with shop signs and walkers in the distance](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/29.webp)

### From the court painters' quarter to a cultural district

Insadong-gil runs from the Anguk intersection in the north towards Jongno 2-ga in the south. Gukje News's 2026 Insa Art Week coverage notes that the Joseon royal painting bureau, the **Dohwaseo**, stood near today's Anguk intersection. In modern times antique dealers, art sellers and secondhand bookshops gathered here; according to the Kookmin Ilbo, **Tongmungwan**, a used bookshop opened in 1934, is still run by the third generation of its family.

| 2003 | Car-free hours extended to weekends | Asia Economy, 2011 |

### Change in numbers

The Kookmin Ilbo (16 September 2026) quoted **Jongno District's facility count for the Insadong cultural district**, comparing the year of designation with last year:

| Trade | 2002 | 2025 | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Antique shops | 72 | 47 | -34.7% |

Galleries grew, but in the same article long-time traders say rental exhibition spaces have replaced galleries that actually buy and sell art. Where traditional shops left, modern-hanbok shops, cosmetics stores, money changers and chain cafés moved in. And as Maeil Business reported, the district's management plan meant that **getting your make-up done at a hanbok rental shop on the main street was itself illegal**, which is part of why Seoul now wants to loosen the rules.

![The courtyard of an Insadong shopping building. Pink, yellow, green and white hanbok-skirt decorations hang on wires between bare concrete walkways](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/24.webp)

For visitors: Insadong is **an easy place to buy souvenirs and crafts**, but if you want the old antique dealers, brush shops and framers, **look in the side alleys rather than the main street**. The crafts mall **Ssamziegil** (44 Insadong-gil) opens 10:30-20:30 daily per KTO. Tea houses and restaurants are in [what to eat in Jongno](/en/posts/jongno-food-2026-09/).

### Jogyesa and the Lotus Lantern Festival

![The courtyard of Jogyesa temple covered by a canopy of red, orange and blue lotus lanterns, with the main hall and a walking visitor beneath](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/07.webp)

West of Insadong on Ujeongguk-ro is **Jogyesa**, head temple of the Jogye Order, Korea's largest Buddhist order. Korean Wikipedia says it began in **1910 as Gakhwangsa**, became Taegosa in 1938 and **took the name Jogyesa in 1954**; KTO says the seven-storey pagoda in front of the main hall dates from 1930. It is open at all hours.

Around Buddha's Birthday, Jong-ro becomes the stage for **Yeondeunghoe**, the Lotus Lantern Festival, inscribed by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in 2020 (Herald Business). Maeil Business reports that the 2026 festival ran on 16-17 May and the lantern parade **left Heunginjimun at 7pm and ran along Jong-ro to the Jonggak intersection and Jogyesa**. If you visit in spring, check the date: the parade takes over the main street that evening, so plan buses and taxis around it.

## The eastern palaces and Jongmyo: two World Heritage Sites side by side

Walk a few minutes north from Jongno 3-ga and the mood changes completely. Joseon called this eastern palace zone **Donggwol**, "the eastern palace".

| Site | Founded | World Heritage | Later | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Jongmyo Shrine** | **1395**, following the rule "ancestral shrine to the left of the palace, altar of the state to the right" | **1995** | Royal ancestral rite and its music inscribed as intangible heritage in 2001 | KTO, Wikipedia |
| **Changdeokgung** | **1405**, as a secondary palace to Gyeongbokgung | **1997** | First palace rebuilt after the 1592 Japanese invasion (1610); the de facto main palace for about 270 years | KTO, Wikipedia |
| **Changgyeonggung** | **1483**, expanded for three queen dowagers | — | Zoo and botanical garden from 1909, downgraded to "Changgyeongwon" in 1911; **name restored 31 December 1983** | KTO, Wikipedia |

Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung formed a single compound without a wall between them, and Jongmyo lies just to the south. It is worth knowing that Changgyeonggung was an amusement park for some seventy years: according to Wikipedia, the zoo and plants moved to Seoul Grand Park in 1984, Japanese-style buildings were torn down, the corridors were restored, and the palace **reopened on 23 August 1986**.

![An oblique view of Jeongjeon, the main hall of Jongmyo Shrine. A long single-storey building with red pillars runs along a high stone platform, with a wide courtyard of rough paving stones in front](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/35.webp)

### Jongmyo's main hall, reopened in April 2025

Jongmyo's main hall, **Jeongjeon**, is **101m** long by Wikipedia's figure. Newsis (20 April 2025) reports that a 2014 inspection found insect damage, broken brackets and sagging beams, **repairs began in 2020**, and the **49 spirit tablets** kept meanwhile in Changdeokgung's old Seonwonjeon hall **returned to the main hall on 20 April 2025**, with a procession through central Seoul. So the scaffolding you may have seen in 2020-2024 blog posts is gone.

## Sewoon block 4 opposite Jongmyo: this district's 2026 fight

Across Jong-ro from Jongmyo's main gate lies **Sewoon redevelopment block 4**, at the centre of a year-long dispute between the national heritage authorities and Seoul City. The full timeline is in our [Euljiro guide](/en/posts/euljiro-guide-2026-09/); here is what matters if you are coming to see Jongmyo.

| Item | Detail | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance | About 180m from Jongmyo's wall | Le Monde diplomatique Korea, October 2026 |
| Height | Maximum raised from **71.8m to 141.9m**, 20 to 38 storeys, by a Seoul notice in October 2025 | Same, Herald Business |
| June 2026 | Jongno District approves and publishes the revised project plan | Herald Business, August 2026 |
| 19 August 2026 | Cultural Heritage Committee **defers** the plan for preserving ruins on the site for a second time; the same day, residents' 25-billion-won damages suit against the state has its first hearing | E-Daily, Newsis |
| 26 August 2026 | Land ministry weighing a correction order or cancelling the approval, but "nothing decided" | Herald Business |
| September 2026 | SH (Seoul Housing) appeals the deferral (9th); re-review reported for 16 September | Asia Economy, E-Daily |

Seoul says the profit from extra height will pay to tear down the Sewoon arcades and open a green corridor from Jongmyo to Namsan; the heritage agency and culture ministry say the effect of towers right in front of a World Heritage Site must be assessed first. **Nothing changes for visitors yet.** Jongmyo is open as usual and block 4 has not broken ground. Stand on the platform in front of the main hall and look south, though, and you will see what the argument is about.

## Daehangno: a theatre district where the university used to be

![A red-brick theatre by Marronnier Park in Daehangno. Green ginkgo trees frame a wide plaza leading to a stepped entrance with 'Arts Theater' written above it](/images/jongno-guide-2026-09/16.webp)

East of Jongmyo and Changgyeonggung, around [[line:4]] Hyehwa Station, is **Daehangno**, "University Street". The name is literal.

| When | What | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 April 1929 | Horse-chestnut (marronnier) trees planted in the Keijō Imperial University era | KTO |
| **1975** | Seoul National University moves to Gwanak; its liberal arts and law campus becomes **Marronnier Park** | Weekly Kyunghyang, KTO |
| **1985** | The area is named "Daehangno"; in May Seoul calls it a "street of culture and arts" | Weekly Kyunghyang, KTO |
| 4 May 1985-October 1989 | Weekend **car-free street** (stopped for a road widening) | Nocut News, 2019 |
| **2004** | Named a Seoul **cultural district** | JTBC, Dailian |
| 15 March 2024 | Kim Min-ki's small theatre **Hakchon** closes; reopens in July as Arko Kkumbat Theatre | Korean Spirit |

Daehangno's problem rhymes with Insadong's. JTBC and Dailian have long reported that after the cultural-district designation land prices and rents rose until **the small theatres it was meant to protect could barely survive**. Insadong tried to protect traditional shops and became a souvenir street; Daehangno tried to protect theatre and got higher rents. Still, there are shows every evening. October festivals and how to pick shows that work without Korean are in [things to do in Jongno](/en/posts/jongno-things-to-do-2026-09/).

### Ihwa-dong on the west slope of Naksan

Climb the hill east of Hyehwa Station and you reach Naksan Park and **Ihwa-dong**, once famous as a mural village. Herald Business (May 2016) reported that **residents fed up with tourist noise and graffiti painted over the best-known murals themselves** and were booked for property damage. That tells you people live here. The fortress-wall walk and Ihwa village are covered from the Heunginjimun side in [things to do in Dongdaemun](/en/posts/dongdaemun-things-to-do-2026-10/).

## How to do it

Jongno changes hands through the day. Match the plan to the weekday:

1. **From 09:00**: the eastern palaces. Start with Changdeokgung (closed Mondays) or Jongmyo (closed Tuesdays). On Mondays, Thursdays and Fridays Jongmyo admits only at set guided times, so check the slots first.
2. **Around lunch**: walk west from Jongmyo to Nagwon Arcade and Tapgol Park. To follow the 1919 story, walk from Tapgol Park towards the Taehwagwan site in Insadong.
3. **Afternoon**: Insadong-gil, its side alleys, and Jogyesa. The older shops survive in the alleys.
4. **Evening**: three options. A show in Daehangno, a walk in Changgyeonggung (open until 21:00, last entry 20:00), or the Jongno 3-ga tent bars from 18:00.

**Walking around is mostly free**: Tapgol Park, Unhyeongung, Jogyesa, Insadong-gil and Marronnier Park charge nothing; Jongmyo and Changgyeonggung are ₩1,000 (about $0.75), Changdeokgung ₩3,000.

## Crowds and safety

- **The palaces get busier every year.** Yonhap (February 2026) reports **17,814,848 visitors** to the four palaces, Jongmyo and the royal tombs in 2025, a record; News1 says January-April 2026 brought 5.45 million, up 12%, with foreign visitors up 28% to 1.41 million
- During the **7-11 October Royal Culture Festival** the four palaces and Jongmyo all become event venues
- **The Jongno 3-ga night street is short of toilets**, in the district's own assessment. Note where station and arcade toilets are before you go
- **Smoking and drinking inside Tapgol Park are subject to enforcement.** Many older men gather here; avoid photographing faces
- **Ihwa-dong and Wonseo-dong are residential lanes.** In Ihwa-dong residents once painted over the murals because of tourist noise
- The main street can close for the lantern parade (spring), Independence Movement Day and large rallies. Weekend rallies and New Year's Eve closures are covered in the [Gwanghwamun guide](/en/posts/gwanghwamun-guide-2026-09/)

## Who it suits

**It suits you if:**

- You want to see Joseon architecture, a shrine and palaces, in one day (Jongmyo and Changdeokgung are a short walk apart)
- You like walking through the places where history happened, such as 1919
- You want to watch one neighbourhood switch between older Seoul and young visitors as the day goes on
- You want an evening at the theatre or a drink at a tent bar

**It does not suit you if:**

- Hanok lanes are the main point: try [Bukchon](/en/posts/bukchon-guide-2026-09/) or [Ikseon-dong](/en/posts/ikseon-guide-2026-09/)
- Shopping is the main point: try the [Myeongdong guide](/en/posts/myeongdong-guide-2026-09/)
- You need quiet nights: avoid lodgings next to the Jongno 3-ga night street

## In short

- **Jongno**: named after a bell pavilion. Jongno-gu is 23.91㎢ with 17 administrative dong. This guide: Jongno 1-4 ga, Insadong, Nagwon-dong, the eastern palaces and Jongmyo, Daehangno.
- **Joseon's main street**: Unjongga and 881 bays of licensed shops, the Pimatgol back lane → the 1974 subway and 2009 Cheongjin-dong redevelopment erased most of it.
- **Tapgol Park**: where 1919 began; historic site since 1991. **Games, drinking and smoking under enforcement since July 2025.**
- **Nagwon Arcade and Jongno 3-ga**: a late-1960s arcade over a road, Seoul Future Heritage 2013. Jongno 3-ga was **Time Out's number one for 2026**; 77 businesses trade 18:00-04:00.
- **Insadong**: Korea's first cultural district (2002). Antique shops 72 → 47, galleries 105 → 161. Rules being rewritten in 2026.
- **Eastern palaces and Jongmyo**: World Heritage Sites (1995, 1997). **Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung close Mondays; Jongmyo and Gyeongbokgung Tuesdays.** Jongmyo's main hall reopened in April 2025.
- **Sewoon block 4**: about 180m from Jongmyo, up to 141.9m. Unresolved as of September 2026.
- **Daehangno**: university left in 1975, named in 1985, cultural district in 2004.
- **Airport bus [[bus:6002]]**: Jongno 3-ga (exit 11), Jongno 2-ga, Jonggak; last buses to the airport 20:45-20:50.

In one line: **Jongno is a Joseon market street with a park, an arcade, a cultural district and a night market stacked on top of it, and in 2026 each layer is testing a different rule about what to protect.**
