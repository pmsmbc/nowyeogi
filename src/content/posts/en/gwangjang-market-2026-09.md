Gwangjang Market is the traditional market foreign visitors come to most in Seoul. It is also **the place that has been at the centre of Korea's loudest overcharging row for the past three years**.

This post does two things. It tells you what you eat there, and it sets out **what the Seoul Metropolitan Government and Jongno District actually did after that row**. The second part comes entirely from official press releases, not from news coverage.

![The food alley at Gwangjang Market hung with flag bunting, crowds pressing past signs for bindaetteok and sundae](/images/gwangjang-market-2026-09/01.webp)

## Opening hours first

This is the thing visitors get wrong. **The hours differ by section.**

| Section | Hours | Closed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Food Alley** | **Daily 09:00–23:00** | **Open all year** |
| General section (clothing, fabric, dried goods) | Mon–Sat 09:00–18:00 | Sundays |
| Vintage clothing arcade | Mon–Sat 10:00–19:00 | Sundays |

**Come on a Sunday and it is food only** — no clothing, no fabric, no vintage shopping. The reverse holds too: if you are only here to eat, Sunday is fine, and the alley runs until 11pm.

These are market-level figures, though. **The actual hours of individual stalls are not published in any official source.**

## Getting there, and the name

![The red-brick exterior of Gwangjang Market, with Korean and English KWANG JANG MARKET signs mounted on the roof](/images/gwangjang-market-2026-09/02.webp)

The address is **88 Changgyeonggung-ro** in Jongno-gu, and Jongno District counts **685 shops**.

- **Line 1, Jongno 5-ga Station, exit 8 — 62 metres**
- **Lines 2 and 5, Euljiro 4-ga Station, exit 4 — about 100 metres**

The name is spelled two ways. The government romanisation is **Gwangjang**, but as in the photograph above, **the sign on the market building and the address of the official website both read KWANG JANG**. If one spelling turns up nothing in your map app, try the other.

## What to eat

The Seoul Tourism Organization's official page names these as the market's signature foods: **bindaetteok, modum-jeon, gimbap, sundae, head meat, yukhoe, donggeurangttaeng, nokdujeon and tteokbokki**. The English version of the page adds fish cake.

### Bindaetteok

![A golden-fried mung bean pancake cut into pieces and served on a plate](/images/gwangjang-market-2026-09/06.webp)

The market's signature dish: a pancake of ground mung beans, fried until the edges crisp.

An article in the city's own media outlet gives the origin this way. **Refugees from Hwanghae during the Korean War began frying them in shortening that came out of US army bases, and sold them here.** That article is a citizen-reporter piece rather than scholarship, so treat the story as a story.

### Yukhoe

![A plate of yukhoe topped with an egg yolk, served with garlic and green chilli on the side](/images/gwangjang-market-2026-09/10.webp)

Seasoned raw beef, served with an egg yolk on top. There is **a lane where the yukhoe stalls cluster**, and an article in the city's own media outlet describes it as the yukhoe alley with its long queues. It is not an administratively designated zone, though — it is simply what people call it. Same for the jeon lane.

### Is it mayak gimbap or kkoma gimbap

Here the official sources disagree with each other.

**Seoul's own media outlet says the name was changed.** In its words, public opinion held that **mayak**, meaning narcotics, should not be a friendly word for children, so the dish was renamed **kkoma gimbap**, meaning little gimbap.

**Yet the Seoul Tourism Organization's English page still writes "Mayak Gimbap".**

Both are official sites. The policy basis for the renaming, the date it took effect, and what the signs in the market actually say could not be verified. **It very likely varies from stall to stall.**

## What actually happened after the overcharging row

The most useful part of the post. All of it comes from **the original press releases** of the Seoul Metropolitan Government and Jongno District.

### December 2023 — the quantity-labelling rule

Seoul announced a set of measures to stamp out overcharging at Gwangjang Market, drawn up jointly by the city, Jongno District, the merchants' association and the food-stall traders' association.

- **The quantity must be shown beside the price on the menu**, to stop skimpy portions
- **Sample models** of the signature dishes are placed on display
- Any price increase must first go through a **price-consultation body** led by the merchants' association
- Violators face **suspension of business** and other sanctions

The city's own explainer article gave the example that made the rule necessary. At the time, one stall charged **₩19,000 for 200 g** of yukhoe while another charged **₩28,000 for 300 g**. On price alone the two cannot be compared; with the quantity written next to it, they can. That is a December 2023 illustration, **not a current price** — no current prices appear anywhere in this post, because none are published officially.

On the 19th of that month, the food-stall merchants held a pledge rally and adopted their own rules: **one day's suspension of trading for anyone who skips the training**, a full survey of food prices, and graduated penalties according to the number of violations.

### 2024 — what is on the record in numbers

Jongno District published these figures:

- **A multilingual QR menu system** — **88 food stalls** taking part, giving menus, photographs and prices in **20 languages**
- **A mystery-shopper monitoring team** — **66 Korean and foreign members**, **241 inspections**, **89 items** found to need correction, all fixed on the spot
- **Card payment** — a card payment agency service **newly introduced at 77 stalls** that cannot register as businesses
- **A full stall survey** — the rights holder, operating arrangement and trade name of all **192 street stalls** in the market

### 2025–2026 — the stall real-name system

Jongno District grants **road-occupancy permits under Article 61 of the Road Act**, fixing the area and the term of each stall. It was announced in November 2025, and Seoul says it comes **into force from June 2026**.

The penalties are spelled out. **Demerits are issued according to the number of violations, escalating through suspension of trading up to revocation of the road-occupancy permit.** Lose the permit and the stall cannot trade in that spot.

### May 2026 — the joint inspection

The most recent step. Seoul and Jongno District announced an **intensive inspection through May and June**, becoming a standing regime afterwards.

- **Professional mystery shoppers, including foreigners**, are sent in. **Unfair treatment of foreign customers** is a separate item on the inspection checklist.
- **A joint price-labelling inspection**, covering the **51 categories of retail business** that are required to display selling prices. Serious breaches draw fines.
- **A hygiene inspection**, prompted by a recent controversy over **reused ice**, covering **159 food businesses and 109 food stalls**. Using stock past its use-by date, or failing to display a price label, can mean suspension of business.

### So is it fine now

Honestly: **the enforcement framework is documented, but no figures have been published for how many businesses have actually been suspended.** The results of the May–June 2026 inspection are not out either.

Here is the way to read it instead. **Checking the price and the stated quantity is now the normal, expected thing to do**, and a label that is missing or does not match is grounds for a complaint.

## Practical notes

- **Ask the price first.** With the quantity-labelling rule in place, the menu should state the quantity. If it does not, ask before you order.
- **Bring cash.** The card payment agency service arrived in 2024 and covers **77 stalls**. There is no basis for assuming every stall takes cards.
- **Look for the multilingual QR code.** It is at 88 food stalls. Scan it for the menu, photographs and prices.
- **Weekends are packed.** That is how Seoul's own article describes it, and queues form on weekdays too.
- **On Sundays, only the food alley** is open.
- **You can report a problem.** The grounds are a charge that differs from the posted price, or no price list at all. Call **Seoul on 02-120** or the **tourist information line on 1330**, and bring the receipt or a photograph, the time and the business name — complaints without evidence are not processed.

## A little background

Seoul's own media outlet describes it as **Korea's first permanent market, opened in 1905**, which makes it more than 120 years old this year. The primary-source basis for that claim of "first" could not be verified.

**It appears in Netflix's Street Food: Asia.** The series is from 2019, and Gwangjang is **episode 6, "Seoul, South Korea"**. Netflix's own synopsis says the market's stalls serve up old Korean favourites from soy-marinated crab to knife-cut noodles.

A Michelin listing could not be verified, because the Michelin site blocks access.

## Wrapping up

Gwangjang Market is **worth the trip**. Nowhere else in Seoul has the density of its food alley.

But there is a reason for writing this post the way it is. **The overcharging row really happened, and the authorities really did step in**, and you are better off knowing that before you go. Checking the price and the quantity is not rude here; the system was built to make you do it.

Get that right and the rest is just eating.
