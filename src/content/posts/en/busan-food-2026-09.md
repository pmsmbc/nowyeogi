Busan has a food identity that does not overlap much with Seoul's. It is a port city that took in a huge refugee population during the Korean War, and both of those facts show up on the menu. Prices below are typical ranges as of September 2026.

## The two dishes to plan around

![A stone bowl of dwaeji gukbap, milky pork broth with sliced pork and chives](/images/busan-food-2026-09/05.webp)

**Dwaeji gukbap (돼지국밥)** is pork-bone broth poured over rice with sliced boiled pork. You season it yourself at the table with salted shrimp, chives and a spoonful of red chilli paste, which is why the bowl arrives looking plain. Expect **₩9,000 to ₩12,000**.

The city has over seven hundred shops specialising in it, and Seomyeon has an entire street of them. Menus list variations: *ttaro* keeps the rice separate, *sundae ttaro* adds blood sausage, and *modeum* mixes pork with offal.

![A steel bowl of milmyeon, chewy wheat noodles in cold broth topped with red sauce and egg](/images/busan-food-2026-09/08.webp)

**Milmyeon (밀면)** is the one you cannot get properly outside Busan. North Korean refugees who settled here could not find the buckwheat for naengmyeon, so they made cold noodles from wheat flour and sweet potato starch instead. The result is chewier and slightly sweeter. It comes either in chilled broth (*mul*) or dressed in spicy sauce (*bibim*), and costs about **₩6,000 to ₩8,000**.

## Jagalchi and how the market system works

![Vendors and trays of mackerel and other fish on display at Jagalchi Market](/images/busan-food-2026-09/01.webp)

**Jagalchi (자갈치시장)** is Korea's largest fish market, open **05:00 to 22:00** and closed the **first and third Tuesday** of each month. It is a two-minute walk from Jagalchi Station on [[line:busan1]].

The system confuses first-timers, so here it is plainly. You buy live fish from a vendor on the ground floor. You then carry it upstairs to a *chojangjip*, a restaurant whose job is to slice your fish and serve it with side dishes, lettuce wraps and sauces. You pay the vendor for the fish and the restaurant a separate **per-person table charge**.

That table charge is the part to settle before you sit down, because it is not posted and varies. Widely reported figures are around ₩5,000 a head, with roughly ₩10,000 more if you want the bones turned into *maeuntang*, a spicy stew. Treat those as estimates and ask first.

If that sounds like too much negotiation, the restaurants on the upper floors sell fixed-price sets instead. An assorted raw fish platter runs roughly **₩60,000** for a small to **₩120,000** for the largest, and **hoedeopbap**, raw fish over rice with vegetables, is about **₩15,000**.

## Street food at Gukje Market and BIFF Square

**BIFF Square** is a 428 m pedestrian street in Nampo-dong, named in 1996 after the film festival. It is where **ssiat hotteok (씨앗호떡)** was invented: a sugar-filled griddle pancake, deep fried, then split open and packed with sunflower and pumpkin seeds, peanuts and walnuts. About **₩2,000** each, and the best thing on this list for anyone avoiding meat.

The adjoining **Gukje Market (국제시장)** grew out of the post-war trade in surplus goods and now has a dense food alley. Look for **bibim dangmyeon**, cold sweet-potato glass noodles tossed in red sauce, which is genuinely unique to Busan and hard to find anywhere else in the country.

Gukje Market closes the **first and third Sunday** of each month.

## The rest of the local canon

- **Busan eomuk (부산 어묵)** — fish cake with a much higher fish content than the generic supermarket kind. Sold on skewers with hot broth at markets, and in bakery-style shops by the piece. Samjin Eomuk on Yeongdo has been making it since 1953.
- **Jogae gui (조개구이)** — assorted shellfish grilled at your table, concentrated at Gwangalli and Millak-dong. Reported at roughly **₩35,000 to ₩39,000** for a two-person set, though this comes from restaurant listings rather than an official source.
- **Kkomjangeo (꼼장어)** — charcoal-grilled hagfish, a post-war street food and a classic thing to eat under a tent with soju.
- **Nakgopsae (낙곱새)** — octopus, pork intestine and shrimp stir-fried together in a fiery sauce. A Busan invention and not for the cautious.

## Where to eat by area

**Seomyeon** has the highest density of anything: the pork soup street, grilled meat, and the alley between Seomyeon Crossing and Judies Taehwa. It is also the easiest area to reach from anywhere in the city.

**Nampo-dong** is where the markets are, so it wins for lunch and street food but quietens down at night.

**Gwangalli and Millak-dong** are for shellfish and raw fish with a bridge view, and for drinking afterwards.

**Haeundae Traditional Market** is a five-minute walk from the station and stays open until 22:00, which makes it the convenient option if you are based at the beach.

## Dietary notes, honestly

Busan is a difficult city for restricted diets and it is better to know that in advance.

- **Dwaeji gukbap is impossible to adapt.** Pork broth, pork meat and shrimp paste, with no workaround.
- **Milmyeon is not vegetarian either.** The broth is meat-based and it is usually topped with pork.
- **Ssiat hotteok** is the reliable option: fried in vegetable oil, filled with sugar and seeds.
- **Grilled shellfish and raw fish** work for anyone avoiding pork specifically, though the venues are shared-grill and alcohol-heavy.
- **Halal certified restaurants** are far scarcer than in Seoul. If that matters, our [Itaewon food guide](/en/posts/itaewon-food-2026-09/) covers the country's main cluster.

## Practical notes

- **Markets shut on rotating days.** Jagalchi on the first and third Tuesday, Gukje on the first and third Sunday. Check before making the trip.
- **Carry some cash.** Cards work at most stalls now, but not all of them.
- **No tipping**, anywhere, including at markets.
- **Point and count.** Market vendors rarely speak much English, but numbers and a calculator app get you through.
- **Go early for seafood.** Jagalchi opens at 05:00 and the selection is best in the morning.

## Wrapping up

Eat one bowl of dwaeji gukbap, one bowl of milmyeon and one seed hotteok and you will have covered what Busan does that Seoul cannot. Add a market seafood lunch if you have the appetite and the nerve to negotiate.
